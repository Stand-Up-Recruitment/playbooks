Sales Foundations & Philosophy

Everything you need to know before making a single call: what we believe, how the pipeline works, and how the day is structured.

The Sales Playbook

This isn't just a sales playbook — it's your battlefield guide. Everything you need to open doors, earn trust fast, and close with conviction is right here. From cold outreach to consult calls to locking in clients, every move is mapped with purpose. No fluff, no guesswork — just straight systems built to drive results.

At Stand Up, sales isn't about pushing roles. It's about solving real staffing pain for clients who are bleeding time, money, and manpower. This playbook gives you the frameworks, the mindset, and the tools to lead with confidence and close with clarity.

If you're in the seat, own it. Run the system. Keep the standard high — and let your numbers do the talking.

What We Believe
  • Sales is leadership. We do not beg — we lead.
  • We solve real problems. If they do not have a problem, we move on.
  • We do not sell placements. We sell outcomes: less stress, faster hires, and tradies that actually show up.
  • We do not push — we diagnose. Then we prescribe the right solution.
  • We only work with serious buyers. We qualify hard and disqualify faster.
  • We follow up relentlessly — not to annoy, but to serve.
  • We do not sell on hope. We sell on proof, systems, and risk-free value.
  • We solve people's problems. We are the solution.
How We Sell
  • We interrupt patterns. No boring intros, no salesy garbage. We are real, direct, and disarming.
  • We focus on the client's pain first — not our features.
  • We book meetings, not sell on the cold call.
  • We use frameworks (like Rocket Selling) so every sales call follows a proven flow.
  • We are obsessed with speed: speed to contact, speed to call, speed to close.
  • We build trust by showing how we think — not just what we offer.
What Success Looks Like
  • The client says: “Finally, someone who gets it.”
  • They do not feel sold. They feel understood, helped, and confident.
  • Our deals move fast because we create urgency and eliminate risk.
  • The client sees us as an extension of their team — not a vendor.
Sales success is not about being the most charismatic person in the room. Sales success comes from consistently following a proven system.

Stage 1 — Frame the Call

Script:
“Pretty easy today, I just gotta see what's happening over there and see if we can actually help.”
“If we can help I'll walk you through what we do and if I can't I'll tell you straight away — all good?”

Stage 2 — Clarify the Problem

Open Strong:
“What’s making you look into getting [trade] from New Zealand and not Australia?”
“How long have you been struggling to find good [trade]?”
“What have you already tried to fill these roles?”
“Why do you think that didn’t work?”
“How is not having these [trade] affecting your business?”

(If at any point their answers are too shallow — anywhere in this section — rotate between:) “Can you tell me more about that?” / “Can you give me an example of that?”

“What would having your team fully staffed with [trade] let you do that you can't do right now?”
“How quickly do you want to get these roles filled, is this priority?”

Stage 3 — Label the Problem

Script:
“It sounds like you've got the same problem as most [trade] businesses right now — you've got all the work and no workers.”
“You've been trying to sort this for [time period]. You've tried [what they said], and every time it's [why it failed, their words].”
“And because of that, [pain consequence, their words].”
“And if that keeps going, it's not getting any easier.”
“That about right?”

Stage 4 — The Bridge

Script:
“Cool, look, that's exactly what we solve.”

Stage 5 — Sell the Vacation

Script:
“So what makes us different than everything you've already tried? We open up a whole new country of talent — because you already know you can't find these guys locally. You've already tried, right?” (Rhetorical — no pause, keep moving regardless of response.)
“When we solve the staff issue, what would actually change for you?” (Let them answer, in their own words — reflect back whatever they actually say, don't supply a generic outcome. Pause — genuine space here, let it land.)
“When we fix those things, your business stops leaving money on the table.” (Pause)
“We currently have [trade] ready to go, who are qualified and not being fought over by every other business around you. But most importantly, they've made a decision to uproot their whole lives — moving their families to Australia — which means they show up with a different mindset. They want to join a company long term. They want to be looked after, paid on time, and just show up and do a hard day's work and go home. That's all they're after.”
“How does that sound to you?” (Wait for a real answer — this is a genuine question, not rhetorical.)
“From here the process is simple.”

Stage 6 — Explain the Process
  • We send you CVs that match exactly what you need.
  • You interview the ones you like and choose your preferred candidate.
  • Once the contract is signed, we handle the relocation support so they hit the ground running.

“So that's how it works, easy and straightforward” (pause half a beat, just so they take it in.. then go straight into next line) And here's how we protect you.

“If the hire doesn't work out for any reason — he doesn't have the skills or he just doesn't fit in with the team — we'll replace them. No drama and no extra cost to you.”
“Now we've got that in place for four months, but I can guarantee you it's not going to take you four months to figure out if someone is a good fit, right?”
“That all clear?”

“Most of our clients offer the candidate some sort of temporary accommodation — whether that’s four weeks in an Airbnb, or something like Flatmates.com. Is that something you’re open to for the right candidate?” (Wait for response.)
“Okay, cool, no worries.”

Stage 7 — Price / Payment Model

Script:
“In terms of investment its $17K,
We split this into two payments — one payment made on a signed employment contract and the second a week after they start.”
“Does that payment structure work for you”
“Is there anything stopping us from getting started today?”
“Cool, we're ready to get started then.”

Stage 8 — Advance the Process (The Close)

Script:
“Look, the next step is simple. I'm gonna send you over an email straight after this call. In that email there's two things I need returned before we can get started:”

  1. A signed copy of the Terms of Business, which has everything we just discussed in here, black and white, easy to understand.
  2. A job description — fill that out, put the skills and experience you need this [trade] to have.

“As soon as I get those back from you, we can get started.” (wait for the response)
“Awesome — when do you think I would have that signed copy back?”
“Great — I’ll get that email over to you now. Looking forward to getting you sorted with these [trade] and helping the business grow.”

How to Use This Document

This document is your training guide for every client call. It explains not just what to say, but why you say it and what you are trying to achieve at each stage.

Read it carefully. Study it. The goal is not to memorise lines robotically — it is to understand the purpose behind each stage so deeply that the conversation feels natural.

The call script is the skeleton. This document is everything underneath it.
Stage 1 — Frame the Call

The Rule: Deliver this calmly and casually. Do not sound rehearsed. Wait for the client to agree before moving on.

Why This Stage Matters

The moment a business owner picks up the phone from a salesperson, their guard goes up. They are expecting to be pitched, pressured, or sold something they do not need. Your first job is to disarm that resistance before it builds.

By telling the client upfront that this is a simple fact-finding conversation — and that you will tell them straight away if you cannot help — you immediately separate yourself from every other salesperson they have dealt with. This openness builds trust before the conversation has even started.

The “all good?” at the end is not a throwaway line. It is the first small agreement of the call. When the client says yes, they have mentally committed to having an honest conversation with you. That small yes matters — it shifts them from guarded to open. Do not rush past it.

Transition: Once they say “all good” or any version of yes — move straight to Stage 2. Do not explain anything else yet.
Stage 2 — Clarify the Problem

The Rule: Do not move on until you clearly understand the problem, how long it has been going on, what they have already tried, and what it is costing the business.

Why This Stage Matters

This is the most important stage of the entire call. Everything that comes after it depends on what you uncover here. You are helping the business owner clearly see and articulate the real cost of their staffing problem — many of them have never sat down and actually worked it out.

There are four things you must uncover before moving on: what roles they are struggling to fill, why what they've already tried hasn't worked, how it is affecting their business day to day, and what it is costing them financially every week the role stays empty (you won't ask this directly).

Do not rush from impact to money. The most common mistake at this stage is asking the financial question too early. Before you put a number on the problem, the client needs to fully feel the impact of it. When they say the role costs them $4,000 a week and it has been empty for three months, they have just told themselves they have lost $48,000. You did not tell them that — they worked it out themselves. That is far more powerful than any pitch you could make.

Most clients will not hand you a dollar figure straight away. They will say things like “it's pushing out our lead times” or “we can't get jobs finished quick enough.” That is money — it is just not phrased as money. If the client does not connect that dot themselves, you connect it for them, out loud, before moving on. This is also where you get the number you will use later — if they push back on price in Stage 7, this is your anchor: “compared to how much you're losing a week.”

Branching into the financial: if they talk about output (missed deadlines, slower jobs, turning down work), say the chain back to them directly: “So if jobs are taking longer, that's slower invoicing — less cash coming in each week this stays unfilled. Fair?” If they talk about people instead (crew's stretched, someone's close to quitting), that's a risk, not a direct cost — ask “what would it cost you if this got worse?” e.g. “What would it cost you to replace one of them if they walked?” If you can't tell which bucket it's in, ask “can you tell me more about that?” first.

New addition: “Why do you think that didn't work?” turns this stage into a real Overview, not just a Clarify. A client who says “the agency just dumped a pile of random CVs on us and wasted three weeks” has handed you a far heavier line for Stage 3 than “tried an agency, didn't work.” If the first answer is thin, oscillate — “can you tell me more about that?” / “can you give me an example of that?” — until you've got something specific enough to hand back to them later.

When the prospect goes quiet or vague:

Vague answer
Reflect it back and ask one more question: “When you say tricky — what does that look like day to day?”
Goes quiet
Normalise it and prompt gently: “Take your time — I just want to make sure I actually understand what's going on before I say anything.”
Reluctant on financials
Make it easier by starting small: “Rough ballpark is fine — just so I've got a sense of the scale of it.”
Disengaged or short
Check in directly: “Can I ask — is this something you're actually looking to solve right now, or is the timing not right?” A disengaged prospect who stays disengaged is not worth finishing the call with — better to know early.
Transition: You are ready to move on when you have: the role, the duration, what they've tried and why it didn't work, the impact, and a dollar figure (worked out together, not asked directly). The rule: never move out of Stage 2 with incomplete information — a weak discovery produces a weak close every time.
Stage 3 — Label the Problem

The Rule: The client must confirm your summary before you move into the solution. Do not move forward without that agreement.

Why This Stage Matters

At this point the client has shared a lot of information. Your job now is to show them that you were actually listening — and to organise everything they said into a clear, single picture of the problem.

This does two things. First, it builds trust: when you repeat back exactly what someone told you, in their own words, they feel genuinely heard. Second, it reinforces the pain: when you say out loud “you've been trying for six months, everything you've tried hasn't worked, and it's costing you $4,000 a week” — the client hears it differently than when they were just answering questions. That weight is what creates urgency.

Open with a normalising line, not a diagnosis. Keep the escalation soft, not dramatic — a manufactured worst-case reads as salesy. If it feels forced, it's fine to drop the escalation line entirely and close straight from the pain consequence.

Always end with a confirmation — casual is fine. This is not just a courtesy, it is a deliberate agreement: when the client says yes, they have confirmed the problem is real, the impact is real, and the cost is real. Only use what the client told you — do not add new information, exaggerate, or assume. If you get something wrong and they correct you, adjust and re-summarise.

Transition: Wait for them to confirm. If they correct something, adjust and re-summarise. Only move to Stage 4 once they have said yes — or something close to it.
Stage 4 — The Bridge

The Rule: Keep this short. Do not explain anything yet. This line simply signals that you are shifting from the problem to the solution.

Why This Stage Matters

The call has spent time focused entirely on the problem. That focus was intentional, but now the client has confirmed the problem, and continuing to sit in it would feel repetitive.

This one line acts as a gear change. It signals that something different is coming without giving anything away yet, and creates a moment of forward momentum that makes the client lean in slightly. That lean is what you want going into Stage 5. Do not over-explain here — one clean line is all you need.

Transition: Say the line and go straight into Stage 5 without pausing for a response.
Stage 5 — Sell the Vacation

The Rule: Always use the client's exact words from Stage 2. The script is a guide only — do not list generic benefits. Only speak to what they actually told you.

Why This Stage Matters

Up until now the entire call has been about the problem. This is the moment you shift to the future — what life looks like once the problem is solved. People do not buy services, they buy outcomes.

This is called selling the vacation because when someone books a holiday, they are not buying flights and a hotel room — they are buying the feeling of being on the beach. You are not selling recruitment. You are selling the feeling of having the right people in place.

If you list benefits the client did not mention, it feels like a script. The moment they sense a script, they stop feeling and start evaluating — and only one of those two mental states closes.

Coaching note: before technique, get clear on what you are trying to make the prospect feel. They have just confirmed everything that is wrong — your job now is to help them feel, even briefly, what life looks like when it is fixed. Not explained. Not sold. Felt.

The section opens with differentiation, not the dream outcome — an open loop you don't fully answer yet, which carries curiosity into the next line. “You've already tried, right?” is rhetorical — keep moving regardless of response. This is different from the genuine pause later in the section; reps need to hear both types named so they don't treat every pause the same way.

The dream-outcome question is now asked live, not recited from Stage 2 — it gets them to re-live their own answer in the moment rather than hearing a prepared line handed back to them. Reflect back whatever they actually say; don't supply a generic outcome if their answer is thin. The pause after their answer is genuine — give real space, this is a natural point for them to keep talking.

“When we fix those things, your business stops leaving money on the table” closes the emotional moment with a logical anchor — the dream outcome lands in the gut, this line lands in the head. “We currently have [trade] ready to go” resolves the open loop from the opening line. “How does that sound to you?” is a genuine question, not rhetorical — wait for a real answer.

Transition: Whatever they say to “how does that sound to you?” — move straight into Stage 6. Do not linger.
Stage 6 — Explain the Process

The Rule: Keep the process simple and focused on what the client experiences. Do not over-explain what happens behind the scenes. Immediately after the process, remove risk by introducing the guarantee.

Why This Stage Matters

Many business owners have been burned by recruiters before — paid fees, sent unsuitable candidates, left without support when a hire didn't work out. That history creates a quiet hesitation even if they're excited about what you just said.

This stage has two jobs. First, make the process feel simple — the client only needs to understand their part in it: CVs arrive, they choose, the person starts. Second, remove the risk before they raise it. Bringing up the guarantee unprompted signals confidence and tells the client you've already thought about the hard question and solved it.

The final “right?” after the guarantee turns it from a statement into a question the client has to answer — and they say yes every time. That small agreement makes the price conversation in Stage 7 easier.

The accommodation question is a confirmation, not a cold ask — most prospects already indicated yes, no, or open to it when they filled out the ad form before the call, so you're closing the loop rather than introducing something new. “Okay, cool, no worries” works as the response whatever they say. Accommodation isn't always relevant (a FIFO or mining candidate may not need it at all), so a “no” here is not a blocker — keep it brief and move straight into price.

Common Objection: “What happens if the candidate leaves after 4 months?”

“Great question — some of our best clients asked that before working with us.”
“No one can guarantee someone won't change their mind.”
“What we can control is who we put in front of you — and we sit down with them properly before they start and just before the four month mark to make sure they are committed long term.”
“And if they aren't, we replace them immediately.”
“But the bigger risk to your business isn't what happens after four months. It's what this role is costing you every week it stays unfilled.”
“You mentioned earlier it's costing you about [$X] a week — so if this drags on another four months, that's roughly [$X × 16] gone and you're still in the same position.”
Transition: Once they confirm — move directly to Stage 7. Do not add more explanation.
Stage 7 — Price and Payment

The Rule: Deliver the price clearly and confidently, then stop talking. Do not apologise for it, justify it excessively, or rush past it. If the client raises a price concern, redirect to the cost of the problem — not the value of the service.

Why This Stage Matters

By the time you reach this point, the client already understands the problem, the cost of leaving it unsolved, the outcome they want, and how the process works. Your only job here is to present the investment clearly and let it land.

So many salespeople struggle here because they feel the need to justify the price — over-explaining or listing reasons why it's worth it. This actually creates doubt: it signals you're not fully confident in what you're charging. The client doesn't need to be convinced the service has value — you already did that in Stages 5 and 6. They just need to hear the number delivered with confidence.

Notice the question is “does that payment structure work for you?” — not “do you want to go ahead?” You are asking about the how, not the whether. If the client pushes back on price, do not defend the $17K — take them back to the number they gave you in Stage 2. The real cost is not your fee, it's the ongoing cost of doing nothing.

Coaching note — Objection isolation & hard ask: ask the isolation question first — “is there anything stopping us from getting started today?” — while still live on the call, so any last hesitation surfaces now, when you can handle it, rather than later as a stall or a ghosted email. Only once they've said no does the final line come in. “Cool, so we're ready to get started then” is not really a question at this point — deliver it as a statement, almost rhetorically. Asking a real question here invites them to reconsider something they've already ruled out.

Common Objection: “That's expensive / more than I expected.”

“I totally understand — you want to make sure this is the right investment.” (Pause)
“Let me ask you something — if we put price to the side for a second, do you think we can actually solve your staffing problem?” (Pause. If yes:)
“You mentioned earlier it's costing your business about [$X] a week not having this role filled.”
“So over five weeks, that's roughly [$X × 5] gone — and you're still dealing with the same problem.” (Pause)
“So the real risk isn't the $17K. It's doing nothing while the business keeps losing money every week.”
“Fair?”
Transition: Once they confirm the payment structure, ask the isolation question. Once they say no, confirm with the final line and move straight to Stage 8. If they push back on price, use the objection handle above, then return to the payment question.
Stage 8 — Advance the Process (The Close)

The Rule: Present the next step and stop talking. Do not turn this into a big sales moment. Silence after the close is powerful — do not fill it.

Why This Stage Matters

Most salespeople make the close feel like a big dramatic moment — they build up to it, ask a grand question, and put pressure on the client to decide. That approach creates resistance.

What works better is making the next step feel like the obvious, natural thing to do — because by this point, it should be. The client understands the problem, agrees with the outcome they want, understands the process, and knows the investment. There is nothing left to decide except when to start.

Instead of asking “do you want to move forward?”, you simply state what happens next. Laying out exactly what's needed — a signed copy of the Terms of Business and a job description — makes the next step concrete instead of vague. Then stop talking. Many reps get uncomfortable with silence and start filling it, which reopens doubt the client had already moved past.

The final question — “when do you think I'd have that signed copy back?” — asks the client to commit to a timeline out loud. They'll almost always give you a specific day, which is something you can chase; a vague “yeah sounds good” is not. If they hesitate on the date, that's useful information before you hang up.

Transition: If they say yes — confirm and close out cleanly, then end the call. The closing line ends the call on certainty rather than trailing off after their answer, leaving no gap for doubt to creep in after you hang up.

3.3

Stall Objections

Determine if the stall is genuine or a soft no, then use BAMFAM to keep the deal moving.

Training Context

This usually means either they genuinely need approval, or they are still uncertain about something. The goal is to quickly uncover which one.

Rule: do not immediately accept the stall. First determine whether the other person is truly a decision maker or whether the client is simply hesitant.
Client Says: "I need to talk to my partner / business partner."

Script:
“Totally fair. Makes sense you'd want to run something like this past them. Can I ask you something quickly? What would happen if they said no?” (Pause)

If they say they'd probably still do it: the decision is effectively made — move forward.

If they wouldn't move forward without their partner:
“No worries — can I ask you something, do they know you're struggling with this staffing issue at the moment? And they know how much it's affecting the business? So if there's a solution here that solves that problem… do you think they'd be opposed to you fixing it today?” (Pause)

If still hesitant: “Ok no worries. Just out of curiosity, what do you think their main concern would be?” — this draws out the real objection.

BAMFAM (Book A Meeting From A Meeting):
“No worries at all. When do you think you'll be speaking with them?” (Pause) “Perfect. Let's book a quick follow-up call after that so we can go through everything together. I'll send you a calendar invite after this.”

Email Reframes for Related Stalls

General Manager or partner needs to approve: offer a quick one-pager laying out what's included, how the process works, and how the fee compares to leaving the role open -- or offer a joint call.

Need more time, not a no: acknowledge it, but flag that good candidates don't wait around -- offer to hold a spot in the pipeline if there's a rough timeframe.

The Exit Message (Follow-Up #4): confident, clean, no emotion, no over-explaining. “I've reached out a couple of times with information relevant to your situation and haven't heard back. I'll assume filling the role isn't a priority right now.”

Best Case / Worst Case Framework

A simple three-way comparison for fence-sitters -- best case, worst case, and the real worst case of doing nothing.

When to Use This

Use this framework when a client is still sitting on the fence after objections have been handled. It simplifies the decision by helping the client compare outcomes logically.

Best Case / Worst Case Script

“Totally fair. We want to make sure this is the right fit for you. Can I zoom out for 20 seconds and make this a simple best case / worst case decision?” (Pause)

Best Case:
“Best case, we land you a great [trade]. They hit the ground running, help you take on more work, and start making the business money. In that situation, I know spending the $16k isn't going to be a problem… because you'll make that money back quickly.”

Worst Case:
“Worst case, for whatever reason it doesn't work out. In that situation we replace them, and you're not stuck with a bad hire.”

Real Worst Case:
“But there is one option that guarantees nothing changes. That's doing nothing and continuing exactly as things are now. And you mentioned earlier that's costing around ($X) per week.” (Pause)

“So the decision becomes pretty simple. Does it make sense to take a risk-free shot at solving this once and for all?” (Pause)

5.1

Training & Scaling (Hormozi Method)

How Stand Up trains and scales a sales team using Alex Hormozi's principles -- proof, system, hiring, and the 9-level ladder.

Founder Proves the Process First

Before hiring salespeople, the founder must prove the sales process personally. Salespeople cannot discover the process — they can only execute an already proven process. The founder must first determine who the ideal customer is, what problem they are trying to solve, what objections appear repeatedly, what messaging resonates most, what the offer must look like to convert, and what the closing structure should be.

Training rule: salespeople are not hired to "figure it out." They are hired to execute a proven process. Founder closes deals -> process is documented -> salespeople are trained to repeat it.
Turn the Founder's Brain into a System

Once the founder's sales approach works, convert it into a repeatable structure: call framework, question flow, problem diagnosis process, objection handling framework, offer presentation structure, and close process.

Key rule: if the sales process lives only in the founder's head, the business cannot scale. The sales system becomes the company's intellectual property.
Hiring, Training & Scaling
Hiring Salespeople
Hire for traits, not experience. Experience does not predict success. Traits that matter: work ethic, coachability, competitiveness, emotional resilience, hunger for success. People with these traits can be trained; people without them cannot.
Sales Training System
Training is built around real sales calls. Every call should be recorded. Identify the best calls, break down why they worked, and show new hires exactly what success looks like -- this shortens the learning curve dramatically.
Performance Scoreboards
Sales teams must operate like sports teams -- performance must be visible. Every salesperson should see calls made, meetings booked, deals closed, revenue generated. Leaderboards create competition; competition drives performance.
The 9-Level Sales Ladder
To retain top performers, promotions must happen frequently. Junior: Levels 1-3. Intermediate: Levels 4-6. Senior: Levels 7-9. Each promotion includes increased responsibility, compensation, and higher expectations. Employees should feel roughly six months from the next promotion.
Performance Management
Track performance weekly, coach underperformers quickly, set improvement targets, and remove persistent low performers. A team of high performers will outperform a larger team with weak members.
The Sales Scaling Formula
  1. Founder proves the sales process
  2. The process is documented and scripted
  3. Salespeople are hired for traits
  4. Training is built using real call recordings
  5. Performance scoreboards drive competition
  6. Promotions are structured using the nine-level ladder
  7. Underperformers are removed
This creates a predictable sales engine -- transforming sales from a founder-dependent activity into a repeatable system that any trained salesperson can execute.

Script Memorisation Method
  1. Print two copies of the script
  2. Read the full script out loud
  3. Black out one word with a marker
  4. Say the script again -- if they stuff up the blacked-out word, look at the clean copy and restart
  5. When they get it right, black out the next word
  6. Repeat until every word is blacked out
A 400-word script means they say it 400 times. By the end they don't think about the words -- they just say them. This is what allows natural delivery, real listening, and confident tone.
