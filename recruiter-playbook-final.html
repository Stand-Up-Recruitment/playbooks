Job Creation

Everything starts here. No recruiter activity begins until a job is live in JobAdder — this is the rule that keeps the whole process clean.

Tell — What You Need to Know

A new job at SUR starts when the sales team closes a client. This means a business in Australia has agreed to work with us to find them a tradesperson. Once that happens, two documents are created and sent to you:

  1. The client job description — sent to you via email. This is the document the client fills out telling us what kind of person they are looking for, what the role involves, and what they are paying.
  2. The sales call transcript — this is a recording of the conversation between Les and the client. The EA uploads it to SharePoint so you can access it.

Your job in this stage is to take those two documents and turn them into three things:

  1. A job brief — a structured internal document that tells you everything you need to know to recruit for this role
  2. A job card in JobAdder — the official record of the job in our recruitment system
  3. A job ad — the public-facing advertisement that candidates will see on job boards
This is the starting gun for recruitment. Nothing happens before this stage is complete.

AI Sourcing

Sourcing is how candidates enter your pipeline. Your AI bot calls candidates on your behalf every day. Your job is to tell it what to look for each morning — it does the rest.

Tell — What You Need to Know

Sourcing is how candidates enter your pipeline. The goal is simple - find qualified New Zealand tradespeople who are keen to move to Australia and get them booked in for an internal interview. You have two ways to do this: AI sourcing and manual sourcing. Use both every day.

Each recruiter at SUR has a dedicated AI bot set up by our Automation Team. The bot's job is to call candidates from our database, screen them on the basics, and book qualified candidates directly into your calendar for an internal interview.

By the time a candidate appears in your calendar, they have already been called and agreed to meet you. Your job at this stage is to submit the sourcing form every morning to tell the bot what roles to source for that day.

The bot does not remember what it called yesterday. You must submit the form every day for every active role you want sourced.
What the bot does
Calls candidates from the database, screens them on basic eligibility, and books qualified candidates directly into your Calendly. The booking appears as "Stand Up Recruitment — Internal Interview" with the candidate's name, date, time, and location already filled in.
What you do
Fill out the Airtable sourcing form each morning, check your calendar throughout the day for new bookings, and check in with Trung if any candidates hung up or did not answer so you can follow up manually.
Full Process Breakdown
Using a Boolean search string in the Role field

Instead of typing a single job title in the Role field, you can use a Boolean search string to capture a wider pool of candidates. This expands the AI bot's parameters so it isn't limited to an exact title match — it can pick up related trades, alternate titles, and common variations.

Run the Boolean Search String Generator prompt (see Prompts tab) in Claude, copy the output, and paste it into the Role field on the Airtable sourcing form instead of a single job title.

Example: For a carpenter role: "Carpenter" OR "Chippy" OR "Framer" OR "Residential Builder". This tells the AI bot to widen its net across all of these related titles rather than only calling candidates who have "Carpenter" listed exactly.
Step 1 — Fill out the Airtable sourcing form every morning

Open the Airtable sourcing form: airtable.com/appa05OOLs3y4DF14/pagUZmYnoV1iGIZLd/form

Fill in the following fields:

  • Role — the job title you are sourcing for (e.g. Electrician, Carpenter, Welder)
  • Job Description — a short plain English prompt telling the bot what kind of candidate you want
  • Recruiter — select your name from the dropdown

Submit the form. If you are sourcing for multiple roles, submit a separate form for each role.

Prompt formula: "I want to source for a [trade/role] that [key requirement] and [availability or location preference]."
RoleGood prompt
ElectricianI want to source for a qualified electrician that is keen to move to Australia in the next couple of months.
CarpenterI want to source for an experienced carpenter with residential framing experience that is open to relocating to Australia.

Keep it to one or two sentences. The AI bot's job is to call and book — you do the thorough interview. Do not write long paragraphs or detailed requirements.

Step 2 — Check your calendar for bookings

Throughout the day, check your calendar. When the AI bot successfully books a candidate, a new event will appear titled "Stand Up Recruitment — Internal Interview" with the candidate's name, date, time, and location already filled in. You do not need to confirm these — they are already set.

Step 3 — Check in with Trung for manual follow-ups

Some candidates will hang up, not answer, or the call may not connect. These are leads that should not be wasted. Each day, check in with our Automation Team to find out which candidates hung up or did not answer. Add these to your manual sourcing list and follow up yourself in Stage 2.2.

A qualified NZ tradesperson who wants to move to Australia is a valuable lead. Do not let them slip through because the bot did not connect. If Trung flags a hang-up on a role you are actively hiring for, call them yourself.
Common Mistakes
Forgetting to submit the form — the bot does not carry over from the previous day
Submitting one form for multiple roles — submit a separate form for each role
Writing a long or complicated job description prompt — keep it to one or two sentences
Ignoring hang-ups and no-answers — these are leads worth following up manually

Manual Sourcing

The AI bot covers a lot of ground but it cannot do everything. Manual sourcing is how you go after candidates the bot missed, candidates who need a human touch, and fresh leads from Trade Me and the cold email database.

Tell — What You Need to Know

Manual sourcing runs alongside AI sourcing every day. There are three channels:

  1. JobAdder candidate database — candidates already in our system who match the trade you are hiring for
  2. Cold email outreach — reaching out to past candidates in our Excel database via email
  3. Headhunting on Trade Me JobSmart — finding fresh candidates on Trade Me's database and unlocking their profiles

Your goal in each channel is the same — identify qualified NZ tradespeople who are keen to move to Australia and either book them for an internal interview or add them to your pipeline for future roles.

Keep your job brief open every time you source. It is your filter for every single call and message.

Candidate Status
Update this every time you call someone

Every candidate profile in JobAdder has a status field. This status determines whether the AI bot or another recruiter will call this candidate again, so it must be kept accurate every single time you make a call — even if the candidate doesn't qualify for anything right now.

StatusWhen to use it
New / UnscreenedThe default status for all new candidates. Do not leave it here after you've called them.
In ProcessThe candidate is being progressed in any job card in JobAdder.
Screened - ActiveYou (or the AI) have spoken to the candidate and logged notes, but there is no current job that matches them. Use this status when a candidate doesn't qualify for the specific role you called about but is otherwise a fine candidate for future roles.
Unsuccessful - InternalThe candidate failed the screening call or the internal interview — not a serious issue, just not the right fit this time.
Unsuccessful - ClientThe candidate failed the client interview.
Placed / Currently EmployedSelf explanatory — the candidate has been placed.
Do Not Use / BlacklistedFor serious cases only — rude, unreliable, or someone we do not want to work with again.
CalledThe candidate did not answer your call.
Never click Reject on a job card. Rejecting a candidate directly from a job card automatically changes their overall status to Do Not Use / Blacklisted — even if they were simply not right for that specific role. This is a system quirk, not something we want. Always go to the candidate's profile and manually update their Status field instead. If a candidate is a duplicate across multiple job cards and you have moved them forward on one, just leave the other applications as they are — no action needed on those.

If a candidate doesn't qualify for any current job, do not leave their status as New / Unscreened. Update it to Screened - Active and log your notes. This tells the AI bot and other recruiters that this candidate has already been spoken to, so they are considered correctly for future roles rather than called again from scratch.

The Screening Call Script

Keep it conversational. These questions do not need to be asked in order but you must cover all of them by the end of the call.

Opening: "Hey [Name]! It's [Your Name] from Stand Up Recruitment. You showed interest in work in Australia, is that right?"
#QuestionScript
OpeningWhy do they want to move?"What's making you want to make the move to Australia?"
Q1Skills and experience"I can see that you are a [trade]. Can you expand a bit more on your skills and experience?" — let them talk, listen actively, then ask the specific qualification questions for the role
Q2Readiness to move"And when are you looking to make the move?" — acknowledge and note their timeline
Q3Trade-specific qualificationsAsk the relevant qualification questions based on the role brief — e.g. "Are you qualified in that trade?", "Do you hold a current [specific ticket]?"
Q4Anything to add"Great! Is there anything else you would like us to know or anything you would like to add?"

If not suitable: "Awesome, thanks so much for chatting with me today [Name]. We will be in touch if anything comes up that matches your experience. Have a good one!"

If no answer — send the SMS template: "Hi [Name], it's Ian from Stand Up Recruitment. You showed interest in moving to Australia for work. I've got some exciting opportunities for [Trade] I think you'd want to hear about. When are you available?"

How to Use This Guide
The ethos behind every script in this guide: every interaction - whether it's a candidate, a referee, or a client - is a conversation, not a form you're working through. Build rapport. Be genuinely curious about the person in front of you. Ask follow-up questions because you actually want to know the answer, not because a script told you to. The words matter less than the mindset you bring into them.
Screening Call
1. The Open - Confirm Interest, Not Interrogate
Sound like you're following up, not cold calling.

This isn't a cold call and it shouldn't feel like one. The candidate already raised their hand - they showed interest somewhere along the line. Your opening line is just confirming that, warmly, like you're following up on something they started, not starting something yourself.

You: Hey [Name]! (pause) It's [Your Name] from Stand Up Recruitment. (pause) You showed interest in work in Australia, is that right?

Pause after their name and after you've said who you are. These two pauses give them a beat to register that this is a real person on the line and place who you are, before you ask them anything. Skip the pauses and the whole line runs together - they're still processing "who is this" while you've already moved on to the question.

Candidate: Yeah, that's right.

Say it like you already know the answer is yes. If they sound confused or say no, don't panic - just clarify where the interest came from (an ad, a database entry, a referral) and reset from there.

2. The Why - Get Them Talking About Themselves
Don't accept the first answer - dig one layer deeper.

This is the most important question in the whole call, and it's also the one recruiters rush past the most. Don't ask it and move on the second you get an answer. A generic answer like "better money" or "just want a change" is the start of the conversation, not the end of it.

You: What's making you want to make the move to Australia?

Let them talk. Don't jump in with the next question the second they pause - that pause is often them thinking of more to say. If they give you something surface-level, gently dig one layer deeper: "Yeah? What's driving that for you at the moment?" or "Is there something specific behind that?" You're not interrogating them, you're just genuinely curious. People can tell the difference, and it's the difference between a transactional call and one where they actually open up.

3. Skills and Experience - Let Them Sell Themselves
Show them you've paid attention before you ask anything.

Frame this as recognition, not a request. "I can see that you are a [trade]" tells them you've already looked at their profile - it shows you're paying attention, not running through a checklist.

You: I can see that you are a [trade]. Can you expand a bit more on your skills and experience?

Let them run with this. If they're vague or give you a one-liner, prompt gently - "What kind of projects were you working on day to day?" or "What's been the biggest job you've handled recently?" Once they've given you a general picture, that's your cue to ask the specific qualification questions for the role you're screening for.

4. Readiness to Move - Anchor the Timeline
Acknowledge before you move on - don't just log it and go.

This is a practical question but it still needs warmth. You're not checking a box, you're trying to understand where they're actually at in their head - some people have already made peace with moving, others are still testing the idea out loud for the first time.

You: And when are you looking to make the move?

Acknowledge whatever they say before moving on - don't just note it down and jump to the next question. A simple "Okay, that's good to know" or "Right, that gives us something to work with" keeps it feeling like a conversation rather than data collection.

5. Trade-Specific Qualifications - Confirm the Non-Negotiables
The one part that's closer to a checklist - keep the energy up anyway.

This is the one part of the call that is genuinely closer to a checklist, and that's fine - the candidate expects some practical questions. Just don't let the tone shift into interrogation mode. Keep the same conversational energy you've had the whole call.

You: Are you qualified in that trade? Do you hold a current [specific ticket]?

Ask the specific tickets or qualifications relevant to the role brief. Acknowledge their answers before moving on - even a quick "Perfect, that's exactly what we need" keeps the energy up.

6. Anything to Add - Leave the Door Open
Don't skip this - the best information often shows up here.

This question often gets skipped because it feels like filler, but it's not. It gives the candidate a chance to raise something you didn't think to ask about, and it signals that you're interested in them as a whole person, not just the boxes you needed ticked.

You: Great! Is there anything else you would like us to know or anything you would like to add?

Whatever they say here, take it seriously - sometimes the most useful information in the whole call comes out in this moment because the pressure of "being asked questions" has lifted.

7. The Close - Candidate Is Suitable
Sell the next step before you ask for it. Then book it before you hang up.

A lot of recruiters jump straight to "can we book a call" without giving the candidate a reason to say yes. Give them a hook first - a genuine reason this is worth their time - then ask. And once they say yes, do not let the call end with a vague "we'll be in touch." Lock in a real day and time before you hang up, every time.

You: Look, we've got some great opportunities over in Australia at the moment - better pay, better hours, real stability for you and your family. I'd love to jump on a quick video call or meet in person to run you through it properly. Are you free tomorrow at [time]?

Adjust the specifics to whatever is genuinely true for the role you're sourcing for - don't force in generic selling points if they don't apply. The hook only works if it's honest. Confirm a day and time on the call itself. Aim for same day or next day. Book it directly in JobAdder before you move to your next call.

8. The Close - Candidate Is Not Suitable
This is their last impression of us - make it a good one.

Say it slow and stay warm. This person gave you their time and their story - the way you close the call is the last impression they have of Stand Up Recruitment, so make it a good one.

You: Awesome, thanks so much for chatting with me today [Name]. We will be in touch if anything comes up that matches your experience. Have a good one!

Update the candidate's status straight after the call while it's fresh in your mind - don't let it wait until end of day.

Internal Interview

This is the most important conversation you have with a candidate before they meet the client. Get this right and everything after runs smoothly.

Tell — What You Need to Know

The internal interview is conducted either in-person at the office or via Microsoft Teams — the process is exactly the same regardless of format.

How the interview opens depends on how the candidate came into your pipeline:

AI sourced
The AI bot screened them but did not disqualify anyone. The recruiter must reconfirm experience and readiness at the start before investing time selling the opportunity. If they do not pass this check, close the call professionally and move on.
Manually sourced
The screening call already qualified them. Lead with the opportunity straight away.

After that initial difference, both paths follow the same flow through to the close.

Prepare your technical questions before the interview. Never wing this one.

Part 0 — Uncover the Real Why

This is the first real question in every internal interview, regardless of how the candidate came in or whether it's video or in-person. Les is firm on this — surface-level answers are not good enough. Every candidate gets asked why they want to move, and every candidate gets 2 to 3 follow-up questions to dig into what is actually driving that answer.

"What's making you want to make the move to Australia?"

Most candidates will give you a surface answer first — better money, better weather, a change of scenery. That is not the real answer. Your job is to keep asking until you get to what is actually behind it.

Example: Candidate says: "I want to move because the money is better." Do not accept that and move on. Ask 2 to 3 follow-up questions such as: "Sure, but what would that extra money actually let you do that you can't do right now?" / "Is there something specific driving that - a house deposit, supporting family back home, paying off debt?" / "Why does that matter to you at this point in your life?"

Keep digging until you get a real, specific answer — not a generic one. This tells you whether the candidate is genuinely motivated to make this move work or just testing the water. It also gives you something real to reference later when selling the opportunity and during the cool off period conversation. Log the candidate's real motivation in your interview notes. This context matters for the rest of the process.

Internal Interview
0. Uncover the Real Why - Before Anything Else

Les is firm on this one. Every candidate gets asked why they want to move, and every candidate gets 2 to 3 follow-up questions to actually get to what's driving it. This happens first, before you sell anything, before you reconfirm anything - it's the foundation the rest of the conversation sits on.

Do not accept the first answer. This is the question the whole call is built on.
You: Hey [Name], good to meet you - thanks for jumping on.

Open warmly regardless of how the candidate was sourced - this greeting only happens once, right here. You won't repeat it again once the paths split below.

You: What's making you want to make the move to Australia?

Most candidates give you a surface answer first - better money, better weather, a change of scenery. That's not the real answer, it's the doorway to it. Your job is to keep gently pushing until you get something specific and real.

Example: Candidate says: "I want to move because the money is better." Do not accept that and move on. Ask 2 to 3 follow-up questions such as:
  • "Sure, but what would that extra money actually let you do that you can't do right now?"
  • "Is there something specific driving that - a house deposit, supporting family back home, paying off debt?"
  • "Why does that matter to you at this point in your life?"

Keep digging until you get a real, specific answer - not a generic one. This tells you whether the candidate is genuinely motivated to make this move work, and it gives you something real to reference later when you sell the opportunity and during the cool off period conversation. Log their real motivation in your notes - you'll use it again.

After Part 0, the interview splits based on how the candidate came in.

Path A - AI Sourced Candidates: Reconfirm First
The AI bot doesn't disqualify anyone - that job falls to you, right here, before you sell a thing.

If the candidate came through AI sourcing, the bot has already had a conversation with them but it doesn't screen anyone out. That means the qualification check that would normally have happened on a screening call hasn't happened yet. You need to do it now, before you invest any energy selling the role. Keep it warm - this isn't an interrogation, it's just doing the check that hasn't been done yet.

You: Before I run you through what we have, I just want to quickly confirm a couple of things on your end.

1Are you a New Zealand citizen?Hard requirement - non-citizens cannot proceed
2What trade are you in and what have you been working on recently?Confirms their experience is relevant

4When are you looking to make the move?Checks timeline alignment

If they pass, continue to Part 1 - Run Through the Opportunity. If they do not pass:

You: Thanks for your time [Name] - based on what you've told me, we're not quite the right fit at this stage but we'll keep you in mind if something changes.

Path B - Manually Sourced Candidates: Lead With the Opportunity
Screening already happened. Don't repeat it - build on it.

If the candidate came through manual sourcing, they've already been through a screening call, so asking the same qualification questions again just wastes their time and makes the process feel repetitive. Go straight into selling the role.

You: This is just a quick chat so I can give you a proper rundown of the role and see if it's a good fit for where you're at.
1. Run Through the Opportunity
Sell it like you actually believe in it - because the parts that are true are genuinely exciting.

Use the Selling Points section of the job brief as your foundation, but don't read it out like a list. Weave in what you learned from Part 0 - if their real "why" was providing for their kids, talk about the lifestyle and stability angle more than the pure pay number. Make it feel like it's built for them specifically.

Cover: company overview, role duties, pay and earning potential, type of work and projects, growth and progression, lifestyle in Australia.

You: From what you've told me, I think this could be a really good fit for you. It's not just a job - it's a genuine opportunity to step up, earn well, and set yourself up properly.
2. Candidate Confirms They're Keen
Don't assume interest - check it before you go deeper.

This is a short but important checkpoint. If they're hesitant here, it's worth understanding why before you invest more time in the technical and financial parts of the conversation.

You: Does that sound like something you'd want to hear more about?

If yes, continue. If no or hesitant, understand their concerns before proceeding.

3. Screening Questions
Go deeper than a checklist - but this part is genuinely more practical, and that's fine.

These questions are more transactional by nature, but keep the same conversational warmth you've had the whole call. If anything disqualifying comes up here, it's better to find out now than after the client interview is booked.

  • If you were to move, what's your timeframe?
  • Would you be moving over on your own, or bringing family with you?
  • We don't need to know your criminal history, but is there anything serious that could stop you from moving to Australia for work?
  • Any medical conditions or restrictions we should be aware of that might affect site work?
  • Do you have a valid passport with at least 6 months validity?
  • Do you have any planned vacations or commitments in the next few months?

If anything disqualifying comes up, close the call professionally before continuing.

4. Financial Readiness Discussion
This exists to protect the candidate, not to interrogate them. Frame it that way.

This conversation exists to reduce dropouts after the offer - but if you frame it as "we need to check you can afford this," it feels transactional and a bit cold. Frame it instead as looking out for them: you'd rather they know the real numbers now than get blindsided later. That framing changes everything about how this lands.

Walk the candidate through the real costs - flights, accommodation, and a car, landing at roughly $5,000 all up to be comfortable.

You: Moving over costs more than most people expect. Your flight alone is $500 to $1,000. Accommodation runs $350 to $850 a week. Most people need a car within the first few weeks. So realistically, before your first pay comes in, you're looking at around $5,000 all up. (pause) Does that align with where you're at financially?

Pause before asking if it aligns. You've just given them a genuinely confronting number - rushing straight into the question doesn't give them a chance to actually process it. Let it sit for a second.

Path 1 - Clearly ready: "Perfect. We also offer a relocation package if you want it handled for you - I'll send you the info. Totally optional. Right, let's keep going..."

Path 2 - Unsure: "I appreciate you being straight with me. Use the cool off period to sit down and work out the numbers - I'll send you a cost guide."

Path 3 - Clearly not ready: "Thanks for being honest. If the finances aren't there right now it's going to be tough to make this work. Let's pause, you get yourself in a better position, and we reconnect when the timing is right."

Whichever path they land on, thank them for being straight with you. Candidates who are honest about money now are the ones who don't fall over later - reward that honesty with warmth, not disappointment.

5. Work History and Technical Assessment
Let them talk about their actual work - this is where their real expertise comes through.

This is often where candidates relax the most, because they're talking about something they know well and are proud of. Let them run with it, and use your prepared technical questions to go deeper rather than firing them one after another like a quiz.

You: Talk me through your experience - what have you been working on recently? Walk me through the last role or two.

Listen carefully, ask clarifying questions, and note any gaps against the job brief must-haves. Then ask your 2 to 3 prepared technical questions.

You: What motivates you to work? What gets you out of bed in the morning?
6. Close
Wrap up on a high note, but make sure nothing gets left hanging.

There are three things to cover here and none of them can be skipped - the cool off period, referees, and police check details. Do this in one clean sweep at the end rather than scattering it through the call. End the call with the same energy you opened it with - this is exciting for them, don't let the admin at the end flatten that.

Cool off period:

You: Before we move you to the client interview stage, I want you to take the next 2 days to properly think this through. Relocating to Australia is a big decision. (pause) I'll send you an email with a summary of the role and the relocation information. We'll touch base in 2 days and decide together whether to move forward.

Pause after acknowledging it's a big decision before moving into the practical next steps - let the weight of the decision land before you talk logistics.

Referees: Ask for 2 previous supervisors - name, company, and contact number for each. Stay on the call to collect these. If the candidate says they'll send them later, let them know the details need to come directly to you.

Police check details: Let the candidate know you'll need their full name, email, date of birth, gender, contact number, and residential address.

Do not progress the candidate during the cool off period. Wait for the 2-day follow-up call.

Keep it casual. You want the referee to open up naturally — not feel like they are being formally interviewed.

How to Use This Guide
The ethos behind every script in this guide: every interaction - whether it's a candidate, a referee, or a client - is a conversation, not a form you're working through. Build rapport. Be genuinely curious about the person in front of you. Ask follow-up questions because you actually want to know the answer, not because a script told you to. The words matter less than the mindset you bring into them.
Client Interview
1. The Open - Introduce, Then Get Out of the Way
Set the tone in one breath, then step back.

Your job in the opening is to make the introduction feel natural and then hand the floor over. Don't linger here - the longer you talk, the less this feels like the candidate and client's conversation.

You: [Client name], this is [Candidate first name]. [Candidate], this is [Client name] from [Company]. (pause) I'll let you two get into it - I'm just here to help the conversation along if needed.

Pause briefly after the introductions before handing it over - it gives both sides a beat to actually look at each other and register who's who, rather than being talked over into the next thing. After the introduction, hand over the direction of the interview based on whether this is a new or existing client.

If it's a new client:

You: [Client name], how would you like to run this - would you want to tell [Candidate] a bit more about the company first, or would you prefer [Candidate] walks you through his experience to start?

New clients haven't worked with us before, so giving them the choice up front shows respect for how they like to operate and sets a collaborative tone from the very first minute.

If it's an existing client:

You: [Candidate], give [Client] a quick rundown on your skills and experience.

With an existing client, you already know their style and they already trust the process - you can move straight into the candidate's rundown without needing to check in on format first.

2. Reading the Room - When to Step In for the Candidate
Watch for the moment they need a lifeline, not the moment they need rescuing.

There's a difference between a candidate who's thinking before they answer and a candidate who's actually stuck. Give them the space to work through it first - jumping in too early undermines them in front of the client. Step in only when it's clear they need help getting the good stuff out.

You: [Candidate], you mentioned to me that you can do [x, y, z] - can you walk [Client] through how you approach that on site?

Frame it as prompting a memory, not correcting a wrong answer. This lets the candidate save face and still land the point.

3. Reading the Room - When to Step In for the Client
Sometimes the client needs a nudge to ask the thing they actually want to know.

Occasionally the client will circle around a concern without directly asking about it - commitment, reliability, whatever's on their mind. If you can tell there's an unspoken question sitting in the room, you can surface it for them.

You: [Candidate], [Client] is looking for someone who will commit to at least 24 months in this role - is that something you see yourself doing?

Only do this if it genuinely helps move things toward a yes. You're not creating problems that weren't there - you're clearing up ones that already exist so they can be addressed directly instead of lingering unspoken.

Setting the real stakes - say this in front of everyone: This is a different kind of moment - you're deliberately putting a serious consequence on the table, in front of both the candidate and the client, so nobody can say later they weren't told. It also doubles as a genuine confidence check on the candidate. Slow this one right down.

You: Just one more thing while all the parties are here - [Candidate], if you get over there and you can't do what you've told us you can do, I just want to be completely clear that the client is not going to keep you around or offer you a job. (pause) Are you confident with your skills and the things that we talked about? (pause)

Pause after stating the consequence, before asking about their confidence, and then again after asking it. The first pause lets the seriousness of "not keeping you around" actually land. The second gives them real space to answer honestly rather than reflexively saying yes just to keep the momentum going. Don't run all three into one breath. This isn't about scaring the candidate - it's about making sure their yes is a real yes.

Once the candidate and client are both aligned and confident, close the loop for everyone in the room:

You: With that being said, is there any other questions from either parties?

This is a small line but it matters - it signals the conversation is wrapping up on solid ground, and it gives both sides one last genuine chance to raise anything before you move to the debrief. Don't skip it just because things feel settled.

4. The Debrief - Release the Candidate Gracefully
Get them off the call warmly and without lingering.

This moment can feel a bit awkward if you let it drag. Keep it short, warm, and clear about what happens next.

You: Thanks [Candidate] - we'll be in touch very soon. I'm just going to have a quick chat with [Client] now.
5. The Debrief - Get the Real Answer from the Client
Ask it directly. Don't hedge, and don't fill the space while they think.

Once the candidate's off the call, get straight to it. Clients appreciate directness here more than a soft lead-in - you've both just spent time on this, they know what you're about to ask.

You: What did you think? Does [Candidate] tick the boxes for you?

If they pause before answering, let them. They're forming a genuine opinion, not stalling.

6. If the Answer Is No - Extract Real Feedback
Vague feedback helps nobody. Push gently for something specific.

"Not quite right" or "just not the one" isn't useful - it doesn't tell you anything to adjust for the next candidate. Ask for something concrete, and frame it as helping you help them, not as defending your candidate.

You: Just so I can improve the next batch - what was missing for you?

Whatever they give you, log it properly in JobAdder. This is the difference between your next submission being a guess and being genuinely targeted.

7. Always Leave With a Locked-In Date
Every single debrief ends with a specific date - no exceptions.

This is non-negotiable, whether the answer was yes or no, whether they need to check with someone else or just need a day to think. "I'll get back to you" is not an answer you accept and move on from.

You: No worries - when can we expect a decision by?

Once you have that date, it's live. If it passes without word, that's what triggers the Contract Follow-Up cadence (Stage 9.1) - so getting a real, specific date here isn't just good manners, it's what makes the whole follow-up system work.

Quick Reference - Flow Order:
1
Introduce, then step back - don't linger
2
Read the room - step in for the candidate only if they're genuinely stuck
3
Read the room - nudge the client only if it clears an unspoken concern
4
Release the candidate warmly and quickly
5
Ask the client directly - let their pause sit if they need it
6
If no - push gently for specific, usable feedback
7
Always close with a locked-in date, no exceptions

How to Use This Guide
The ethos behind every script in this guide: every interaction - whether it's a candidate, a referee, or a client - is a conversation, not a form you're working through. Build rapport. Be genuinely curious about the person in front of you. Ask follow-up questions because you actually want to know the answer, not because a script told you to. The words matter less than the mindset you bring into them.
Offer Call
1. Open / Exciting News - Let the Good News Land
This is a big deal for them. Say it like it is.

Don't bury the good news in a wall of process talk. Lead with it, plainly, and let it land before you move into anything practical.

You: Hey [Name], it's [Recruiter] from Stand Up. Good news - we've got your letter of offer through from [Client]. (pause) This is the big one. This means [Client] wants to bring you on board in Australia.

Pause after telling them the offer has come through, before explaining what it means. Give them a second to feel the news before you frame it for them.

2. Explain the Cool-Down Period - Make It About Them, Not Policy
Frame this as something we do for them, not a rule they have to follow.

If this comes across as "company policy," it feels bureaucratic. If it comes across as "we want you to be certain," it feels like we're looking out for them. Same rule, completely different impression - say it the second way.

You: Now before anything gets signed, we give you a 48-hour cool-down period. (pause) That's non-negotiable on our end, and it's there for you. It's your time to sit with the contract properly, talk to your partner or family, and be honest with yourself about timing, location, and what this move actually means for your life. We'd rather you take those 48 hours and be certain than rush it and regret it. So don't sign anything, don't decide anything right now - just go away, read it, talk about it.

Pause after stating the 48-hour period itself before explaining why it exists - the reasoning lands better once they've registered the actual timeframe first.

3. What's in the Email - Set Up the Next Call to Go Smoothly
Tell them why the relocation guide matters - it makes the next call faster for them.

Don't just list what's coming in the email. Explain why the relocation guide specifically matters, because it directly benefits them at the next call - if they've read it, that call moves faster and feels less overwhelming.

You: You'll get an email from us in the next few minutes with three things in it: your letter of offer from [Client], that's the actual job offer, pay, start date, all of it. Our terms of business, standard Stand Up terms, nothing new, just formalising us working together. And a relocation guide, this one's important. Have a proper read of it, because it covers things like your bank details, your tax file number, all the admin you'll need sorted.
You: The reason that one matters: in 48 hours, you're not just talking to me again. You'll be on a call with me and our pastoral care person, and we'll be going through that relocation guide with you step by step. So if you walk in already familiar with it, that call moves a lot faster and smoother for you.
4. Book the Welcome & Relocation Chat - Lock the Date Before You Hang Up
Same rule as everywhere else - don't leave this vague.

Just like every other stage in this process, you don't end the call without a locked-in date and time. This is where you book the Welcome & Relocation Chat.

You: So let's lock that in now - we're talking [today], which means 48 hours takes us to [day]. What time suits you on [day]?
You: Great, I'll send you a Teams invite for [day] at [time]. That's you, me, and pastoral care - we'll walk through everything, answer any questions, and get you moving toward Australia.
You: Take the next two days, talk it through, and I'll speak to you [day].
5. Close on a High Note - Leave Them Feeling Backed
Don't end on logistics. End on how you actually feel about this.

This is a small addition but it matters - the last thing they hear from you shouldn't be a scheduling detail. It should be a genuine, human moment that tells them you're actually invested in how this goes for them.

You: [Name], before I let you go - I just want to say, we're genuinely excited to have you on board. This is a massive step and we can't wait to see you thrive over there. Talk soon!

2.4.1

Contract Follow-Up

Every conversation ends with a locked-in date. Not "I'll get back to you" — a specific date for a decision, a contract, or a next step.

Tell — What You Need to Know

This stage applies whenever a client goes quiet — whether you are waiting on a decision after the client interview or a signed contract after the offer has gone out. The same cadence applies to both situations.

The core rule: every conversation ends with a specific date. If the client goes away to think, your last question before hanging up is always - "When can we expect a decision by?"

The follow-up cadence is not a fixed ladder — it is one rule applied on loop until resolved or escalated.

The Follow-Up Cadence
TriggerAction
Date passes with nothing receivedNext business day - send Email 1
No reply to Email 1Next business day - Call 1
Call 1 produces a new date that is also missedNext business day - send Email 2
No reply to Email 2Next business day - Call 2, then stop and escalate to Les

Every time a new date is given, the loop resets from Email 1. It does not skip to a call.

Weekend rule: if the next action falls on a Saturday or Sunday, it moves to Monday.

Call Prompts

Call 1 — after Email 1 gets no reply:

"Hey [Name], just following up - where are we at with the [contract/decision] for [Candidate]? Anything you need from us to get it moving?" Always leave with a new specific date before hanging up.

Call 2 — after Email 2 gets no reply (hard stop):

"Hey [Name], wanted to get a clear answer today - are we able to get this over the line, or do we need to have a different conversation about timing?" After this call, regardless of outcome, escalate to Les. Do not send further emails or make further calls until Les decides next steps.
How to Escalate & Keeping the Candidate Warm

Default: raise it in the next morning meeting with Les. The EA will follow it up with Les during the day and confirm next steps back to you.

If time-sensitive (candidate about to walk, client finally responded mid-cycle) — don't wait for the meeting. Email Les directly on the spot.

Once the cycle passes Email 1, give the candidate a short heads-up call so they don't go cold or accept another offer while you are waiting on the client.

Common Mistakes
Leaving an interview debrief without a locked-in date — always get a specific date before hanging up
Sending Email 1 on the same day the date is missed — wait until the next business day
Skipping steps — always go Email then Call, never skip ahead
Pushing follow-up actions past the weekend — move to Monday, never skip the step
Continuing to contact the client after Call 2 — stop and escalate to Les
