The Weekly System

What you’ll be doing

Two main things:

  • Lead generation. The paid ads that bring in candidate and client leads.
  • Content. Our social media — videos across Instagram, TikTok and Facebook, plus building Les's personal brand on LinkedIn.

This playbook is your week. It says what you do each day so both keep moving and nothing gets dropped.

Two rules keep it clean: the two shoots never land on the same day (content films Tuesday morning, paid films Thursday), and content that does well on its own becomes our next paid ad — prove it first, then promote it.

What each engine does in a week
  • Plan it (Mon). Decide every post: which pillar, which funnel job.
  • Shoot it (Tue morning). Film with Les — all pillars, one batch, wrapped by midday.
  • Edit it (Wed–Thu). Cut everything for every platform. The big block.
  • Schedule it (Thu). Caption and queue it across Instagram, LinkedIn, TikTok and Facebook.
  • Les's LinkedIn (Thu–Fri). Write his questions Thursday, then ask him in the Friday catch-up.
  • Review it (Fri). Rate what went out, pick the winners.
  • Glance (every day, 2–5 min). Check spend and cost per lead. Look, don't touch.
  • Admin (Mon). Review the numbers, log new recruiter requests, and list what needs filming.
  • Film bank (Thu, as needed). Shoot fresh paid creative with Les — only when the bank's low or there's a new winner.
  • Review + load (Fri). Check for tired ads, swap fresh ones in, and every second week make the call on Proving ads.

NEW — BRIEF Meta Ads — Testing & Scaling: How We Do It (v2)
This brief replaces the old testing/scaling approach for Meta ads. It supersedes the "Phase 1 / Phase 2" content under Still Ads → Step 9 – Scaling wherever the two conflict — this is the current, authoritative version.
1. What changed, and why

The old way: test a few ads, find the winner, put it into scaling, then make variations with slightly different copy and put those variations back into testing. The copy changed between versions.

The problem: we were testing the wrong thing. When copy changes between versions there's no clean read on what actually won — the hook, the script, the location? Every rewrite of working copy also risks killing what made it work.

The new way: test message only. Copy is locked once it wins. Everything that follows — new locations, new formats, trade-specific visual contexts — is built on top of proven copy. We never rewrite a winning script.

The single most important rule: once you find a winning script, the copy never changes again. Only the creative changes.
2. The two phases — Testing and Scaling

There are only ever two phases running at once, with separate budgets and separate rules.

Phase 1 — Testing. One job: find the winning message. Runs on its own small budget, completely separate from everything else. The 70/20/10 rule does not apply here.

  • All ads filmed in the same location, same setup, same everything
  • Only the script changes between ads — that's the one variable being tested
  • Trade name is swapped in each version but the setup stays identical
  • Run until there's a clear signal on which script wins, then stop

Phase 2 — Scaling. Where all proven ads live; the majority of budget goes here. Once a script wins, every creative built from it lives here.

The 70/20/10 rule applies only inside scaling:

SplitCategoryWhat this means
70%Proven winnersCreatives that have confirmed results. Run with the majority of scaling budget. Do not pause them, do not change them.
20%New creatives being provenNew creatives built from the winning script — different locations, formats, trade-specific contexts. Start here, promoted to 70% once proven.
10%ExperimentsNew scripts, new hooks, new angles not yet tested. Small allocation, clear time limit. If they win, they move to testing properly.

Testing budget sits completely outside the 70/20/10. Example: total monthly budget $3,000, $300 set aside for testing — the 70/20/10 applies to the remaining $2,700 only.

The 70% pool is not fixed — it grows over time as new creatives prove themselves and get promoted from the 20%. Goal: a large proven pool so no single creative carries all the weight and fatigue slows down account-wide.

Do not increase budget on a winning ad to scale it — that disrupts Meta's learning phase. Scale horizontally: more creatives, not more spend on the same ad.

3. The one thing we are testing

We are testing one thing only: the message. Not the creative, location, or format — the creative is irrelevant at the testing stage.

What this means for filming:

  • All test ads filmed in the same location
  • Same background, same shirt, same setup throughout
  • Same scripts filmed across all trades — only the trade name spoken changes
  • Edited identically — same pacing, same on-screen text style, same CTA

Think of it like a science experiment — change one variable (the message) and keep everything else identical.

Why identical setup doesn't cause fatigue in testing: each ad goes to a different audience (the plumber ad to plumbers, the electrician ad to electricians). No individual sees the same face in the same context repeatedly. Fatigue is a scaling problem, not a testing problem — we deal with it in scaling through creative variation, not by changing the test setup.

4. How to run a test — the filming session

Three proven scripts, each a different hook style, run across multiple trades to find the winner. The [TRADE] placeholder swaps — nothing else changes.

ScriptHook styleOpening line
Script 1Annoyance Hook"Stop. If you're a [TRADE] in New Zealand, this is going to annoy you..."
Script 2Comparison Hook"[TRADES] in Australia earn up to 50% more than [TRADES] in New Zealand..."
Script 3Six-Figure Hook"If you're a [TRADE] earning under $40, this is for you..."

Filming approach: all test ads filmed in one session, same location/setup throughout. Les films Script 1 saying plumber, then electrician, then welder — then moves to Script 2, and repeats. A full test library from a single morning. The ads will look identical across trades at the test stage — that's intentional. Meta's algorithm reads the trade name in the copy/spoken word and self-selects the right audience.

How long to run tests:

  • Minimum 7 days before drawing conclusions
  • Watch cost per lead — not just click-through rate
  • A high CTR with poor quality leads is not a winner
  • Once one script clearly outperforms the others, move it to scaling

What to film in testing — talking heads only. No trade-specific visual contexts, no action creatives, no props. Hormozi's "put a plumber in a plumber ad" principle applies to scaling, not testing — using trade-specific visuals during testing would make it impossible to tell whether the message won or the visual won.

5. What happens when we find a winning script

Step 1 — Lock the copy. The winning script is permanent — not rewritten, tweaked, or improved. The only change allowed is the trade name swap.

Step 2 — Build new creatives from the same script. Film the same winning script in different creative contexts — different locations, movement, visual setups. This is horizontal scaling: same message, fresh creative wrapper. New creatives go straight into the 20% allocation, not back into full testing. Once a new creative proves itself (matching or beating the original's cost per lead), it's promoted into the 70%.

Step 3 — Add trade-specific visual contexts. This is where Hormozi's visual-identity principle comes in — film trade-specific creative contexts for each trade worth investing in, with the identical script spoken. A plumber watching the sink creative sees themselves in it; that visual identity signal amplifies the message and helps Meta self-select the right audience.

Step 4 — Promote winners, cut what doesn't perform. Any new creative that proves itself joins the 70%. Any that doesn't gets cut — not fixed, not tweaked.

6. Creative fatigue — the real rule

Fatigue is caused by the same face in the same context doing the same thing — not by the same face appearing across many different creatives. Les's face stays consistent across every ad; that's intentional — the goal is repeated recognition so that when a tradie is ready to move, Stand Up is instant top-of-mind. Changing the face to avoid fatigue would destroy the personal brand being built.

What we change to fight fatigue is everything around the face: location, movement, prop, format, visual context. Meta treats each of these as a distinct creative even with the same person on screen.

Ads that are too similar get clustered and delivery gets suppressed. A trade-name swap alone is not enough creative difference at scale — different location and visual context is required.

The whole thing in one breath

A recruiter asks for plumbers → (first time only) we test 3 sets of words to find the best one → we lock those words forever → we make versions of the winner (talking heads, then scenes, then pictures) → we run them and grow a group of winners → once we have about 4 winners we split into two shelves → we keep feeding new versions in and keep the best → when an ad gets old we swap a fresh one from our stock → we only test new words again when the old words stop working.

The big idea — 2 rooms

Think of it like a house with 2 rooms:

  • Testing room — where we try new words (scripts). Only used when we don't know which words work yet.
  • Scaling room — where all our good ads run. This is where most of the money goes.

Inside the Scaling room there are shelves:

  • Proving shelf — a new ad earning its spot. Small money.
  • Proven shelf — the best ads. Big money. These get us the most leads.

Important: at the start you do NOT have two shelves — you only have one good ad. Start with one shelf, fill it up, split it later.

The golden rule: once we find words that work, we never change the words again. Only the trade name and the picture change.
Words you need to know
  • Script — the words the person says in the video.
  • Talking head — a simple video of Les talking to camera. No special scene.
  • Scene version — same words, filmed somewhere that fits the trade (like a sink for a plumber).
  • Still — a picture ad (no video) with words on it.
  • Lead — someone who fills in the form. This is what we want.
  • Cost per lead — how much money it took to get one lead. Lower is better.
  • Control — your best proven ad. New ads are measured against it.
  • The bank — all your filmed clips and pictures, saved and ready to use.

Why we do it this way (Hormozi's "More — Better — New")

Alex Hormozi's big rule: More — Better — New. Do MORE of what works and make it BETTER before ever trying something NEW. New stuff has a learning curve that pulls focus off what's already making money. That rule explains almost everything here:

  • Test the words first, so you can tell what actually worked — change one thing at a time.
  • Lock the winning words, never rewrite — rewriting is "new," and new is the trap.
  • Make versions of the winner — that's "more" and "better." Same words, fresh look.
  • One shelf first, split later — don't split a small budget into two weak shelves.
  • Proving (small money) before Proven (big money) — new ads must earn the big budget.
  • Fix tiredness with fresh ads, not more money — grow with creative, not by burning budget on a tired ad.
  • Judge on leads and real candidates, not likes — once it works, scale it.

One more Hormozi idea behind the whole setup: build the system so it runs without you. If a job isn't written down, automated, or handed off, it's a risk.

Pain Hooks vs Offer Hooks
Where this came from

On Hormozi's "Scale or Fail," he coached a sticker-business owner (Mim) who was capped on ad spend, every time she pushed volume, cost per customer got worse. His diagnosis: her hooks were all product-aware — talking about the offer, aimed only at people already looking for exactly what she sells. That's a small audience. His fix: add hooks aimed at people who feel the pain her product solves, but haven't connected it to her offer yet. That's a much bigger audience, and it's how she could scale spend without CPA blowing out.

The underlying idea: levels of awareness

This comes from marketer Eugene Schwarz. Every person in a market sits somewhere on a ladder — from having no idea they have a problem, up to being ready to buy today. The higher up the ladder, the smaller and warmer the audience. The lower down, the bigger and colder. An ad's hook has to match where the audience actually is, or it either gets ignored (too advanced a pitch, too soon) or it's too small to scale (too advanced a pitch, only reaching the few who are already there).

Our problem right now

Our current offer ("Earn 20–30% more... signed job in 4 weeks... zero cost to you") is aimed at tradies who've already decided they want to move to Australia and are comparing how. That's a small, specific slice of the NZ trade workforce. It converts well, but it can't get bigger — we're competing for the same warm pool every launch, which is why pushing spend just pushes CPA up.

Why this opens up a bigger audience

Pain hooks take longer to turn into a signed candidate than offer hooks do — the person hasn't decided to move yet, so they need the bridge step first. That's expected, not a sign it's underperforming.

  • Run pain hooks cold, to a broad audience.

Writing hooks, scripts + CTAs

Three parts: Hook → Script → CTA. Under 30 seconds (≈ 60–70 spoken words) means every word fights for its place. The rule: make the win look big, the effort look small, real details only. If a line does neither, cut it.

Hook (first 1–3 seconds) — stop the scroll. Name the trade, hit the win or the pain.

  • "Sparkies — you're underpaid, and I can prove it."
  • "NZ plumber? You could be on 30% more in 4 weeks."

Script (the middle — the offer lives here):

  • The win: 20–30% more than your NZ wage.
  • Effort-killers: signed job within 4 weeks · locked in before you leave NZ · costs you nothing · fully supported until you're settled.

That's the whole script. Win + effort-killers. Don't explain the company or list features.

CTA (last line) — one action, name the trade, make it feel fast.

  • "Comment 'AUS' and I'll show you what your trade pays over there."
  • "DM me 'Brisbane' — 30 seconds to see if you qualify."

Full example (≈ 24 seconds):

Sparkies — you're getting underpaid, and I can prove it.

You could be earning 20 to 30% more in Australia, same work. We get you a signed job within 4 weeks — locked in before you leave New Zealand. Costs you nothing, and we support you the whole way until you're settled.

Comment 'AUS' and I'll show you what your trade's earning over there.

Every line is either the win (20–30% more, same work) or an effort-killer (4 weeks, locked in first, zero cost, fully supported). Nothing wasted.

Rules

  • Under 30 seconds. Won't fit? Cut — don't speed-talk.
  • Lead with the trade, never the company.
  • The offer lives in the script — not the hook, not the CTA.
  • One CTA, one action.
  • 6th-grade reading level, NZ tone, sounds like a real person.

Ad Copy/Template

Works on any product or service. Fill the blanks, ship the ad.

THE RULE (read this first)
A good ad makes the win look BIG, the effort look SMALL, and uses REAL details — not vague talk.

Every line does one of two jobs: lift the reward, or lower the effort. If a line does neither, cut it.

Structure is always the same 3 steps:

1. Grab them → 2. Prove it → 3. Tell them what to do.

STEP 1 — Fill your inputs first

Before you write a single line, answer these about your product. This is your ammo.

WHO it's for (the exact person): _______________________________

THE BIG WIN (what they actually want): _______________________________

PROOF it's real (numbers, results,
testimonials, guarantee): _______________________________

EFFORT KILLERS (what makes it easy /
cheap / safe — "we do it for you",
no fees, guarantee, done in X mins): _______________________________

HOW FAST they get it: _______________________________

THE ONE ACTION you want: _______________________________

If you can't fill "Proof" or "Effort killers," the ad will be weak. Sort those first.

STEP 2 — Drop your inputs into the template

PRIMARY TEXT (the big block above the image)

[WHO] — [want THE BIG WIN? ...phrase it as a question or a pain]

[What you do for them in one line]. [EFFORT KILLER]. [We handle the hard bit].

[Qualifier if any]. [ACTION] and [what happens next — HOW FAST].

HEADLINE (bold line under the image, ~5 words)

[THE BIG WIN, squeezed down]

DESCRIPTION (small line under the headline)

[EFFORT KILLER in a few words]

BUTTON

[THE ONE ACTION]

STEP 3 — Worked example (a totally different business)

Proof it works on anything. Here's a mobile car detailing service:

Inputs:

  • WHO: busy Auckland car owners
  • BIG WIN: car looks brand new
  • PROOF: 500+ cars done
  • EFFORT KILLER: we come to you, done in 90 mins
  • HOW FAST: book today, spot this week
  • ACTION: Book

Finished ad:

Auckland car owners — want your car looking brand new without lifting a finger?

We come to you and detail it right in your driveway. Done in 90 minutes. 500+ cars and counting.

Book today and we'll have a spot for you this week.

Headline: Car Detailing At Your Door
Description: We come to you. Done in 90 mins.
Button: Book Now

Notice: every line is either the win (brand new, 500+ cars) or the effort killer (we come to you, 90 mins). Nothing wasted.

Why each part sits where it does
  • Grab (line 1): people scroll past anything not clearly for them. Name the person, then hit the win.
  • Prove (middle): once they want it, their brain asks "will it work for me?" then "is it a hassle?" — answer both, in that order.
  • Tell them (last line): a confused person does nothing. One action, and make it feel fast.

That order isn't random — it's the order the questions pop into the reader's head.

The core principle (read first)

When an ad wins, the temptation is to throw more budget at it — same ad, bigger spend. That's vertical scaling. It works short term, but the ad fatigues fast, CPL creeps up, and you end up dependent on one creative.

We do it differently. Once an ad wins, we lock the copy completely — headline, stat, proof box, CTA — and produce new versions by changing only the visual context. Same words, new look. That's horizontal scaling. Budget per ad stays roughly the same; we just run more creatives in parallel, each hitting fresh audiences or the same audience a different way.

The rule: winning copy is never rewritten. It's only reframed visually. Different background, colour, or format — same message.

This keeps CPL stable long term. At the scaling stage your job isn't to write new ads — it's to produce new visual treatments of proven copy.

Why creative volume is the edge

The business that produces the most creative wins — not because every ad is great, but because volume is how you find winners faster than competitors. While they run the same tired ad from six months ago, we've tested dozens, found what works, and scaled it across trades and formats.

  • 6 — ads per trade in testing
  • 3 — levers to scale each winner visually
  • 1 — rule: copy never changes once it wins

You're not trying to make the perfect ad. You're making enough ads that the winners reveal themselves quickly. The answer is always more creative, not more budget.

Phase 1 — Testing (find the winner)

For each trade, run 6 ads in one campaign: 3 talking-head videos + 3 still images. Same audience (e.g. NZ plumbers), same campaign, same budget logic. Meta spreads spend and the winners surface.

Keep all six in one campaign so you're comparing like with like. Splitting them fragments the budget and muddies the data.

  • Talking head (3) — Les direct to camera, one per proven script (Annoyance Hook, Comparison Hook, Six-Figure Hook). Talking-head only at this stage, no trade-specific visuals.
  • Still images (3) — one per proven script. Black background, yellow callout, white headline, yellow accents. Text-only, no background images yet.

Goal: find one winning talking head and one winning still. Not the perfect ad — the strongest signal to build from.

Do not touch ads during the testing window. Meta needs the full run to exit the learning phase and give real data. Changing anything mid-run resets the algorithm and invalidates the results. Let every ad run the full window (your 9-day cycle) before deciding.
Phase 2 — Horizontal scaling (the three levers)

Once you've got a winning still, scale it straight away. Copy is locked from here. You change only the visual context, using three levers.

Lever 1 — Colour swap (one per trade)

The master set is black/white/yellow (written for plumbers). For every other trade, keep all copy and layout identical and change only the colour scheme.

The only text change allowed is the [TRADE] placeholder. Everything else stays word for word.

Lever 2 — Image-based vs text-only

The master still is pure text on black. Once a winner's confirmed, make a second version with a background image — a tradie on a job site, tools, an Aus work scene — behind the same copy.

Why it works: text-only stops the scroll through contrast and boldness; the image version stops it through recognition — they see someone who looks like them before reading a word. Same message, different scroll-stopping mechanism. Running both finds which the audience prefers.

Sourcing: royalty-free image of a tradie in a work context, with a dark overlay (~80% black) behind it so the white/yellow text stays fully readable. Copy does not change.

The master is square (1:1). Reformat for other placements without changing a word.

Pass/fail — when to cut and when to scale

Every testing ad gets a hard decision at the end of the run. The metric is cost per lead (CPL) only — not CTR, not impressions. CPL is the number that tells you if an ad is producing candidates at a cost that works.

We use our own historical data, not generic industry benchmarks. Set the thresholds before any ad goes live — deciding after you've seen results introduces bias. Write the numbers down before launch.

Step 1 — Pull your historical CPL (Meta Ads Manager):

  • Best-performing talking head CPL / worst-performing talking head CPL
  • Best-performing still CPL / worst-performing still CPL

Filter tip: only count completed campaigns with meaningful spend — ignore anything that ran under the full window or had very low impressions.

Step 2 — Set two thresholds per format:

Step 3 — Make the call at end of the window.

Apply the thresholds when the window closes (9-day cycle). Don't call early on partial data, and don't let an ad run past the window hoping it turns around.

Why our own data: generic benchmarks don't reflect NZ tradies, our offer (job confirmed before you leave), or our setup. Our historical best/worst is the most honest reference, and it sharpens as we run more.

If nothing wins

Sometimes every ad comes in above the cut number. It happens — and it's not the offer or the targeting. In Hormozi's framework the most likely culprit is the hook: the first thing they saw wasn't strong enough to stop the scroll, so nothing else got a chance.

  1. Flag to Les immediately. Don't rewrite anything until you've talked through what the data showed.
  2. Rewrite the hooks only — the callout line and dominant headline. The offer, proof, and CTA stay; they're not the problem. Find fresh ways to stop the scroll for the same message.
  3. Run again — same structure, same campaign logic, same window, new hooks. Keep going until something wins.
No winner isn't a failure — it's data. It tells you those hooks didn't stop the scroll for that audience. The answer is always more creative, never more budget. You don't spend your way out of a hook problem.

THE THREE AUDIENCE TYPES

We use all three, in this order as an account grows.

  1. Cold (interest / broad) — people who don't know us. Where every new campaign starts.
  2. Custom — people who ALREADY touched us (our leads, our page visitors, our video viewers). Warm. Our people.
  3. Lookalike (LLA) — NEW people who look like a custom audience. Cold, but modelled on our best people.

New account = mostly cold. As data builds, we add custom (retargeting) and lookalike.

6. EXCLUSIONS (stop wasting spend)

Always exclude, so we're not paying to reach the wrong people or the same people twice.

The must-do exclusions:

  • Exclude existing leads/clients from cold prospecting sets — don't pay to find people we already have.
  • Exclude candidates from client ad sets, and clients from candidate ad sets — never let the two audiences bleed into each other.
  • On retargeting, exclude people who already converted (booked / submitted) — no point re-selling someone who already said yes.
  • When running a lookalike, exclude the source custom audience — so the LAL finds new people, not the ones already in your list.

Every ad gets the same 5 parts. Only utm_campaign and utm_content change per ad set — the rest are fixed or auto-filled.

PartWhat it tells usValue
utm_sourceWhere they came fromfacebook (always)
utm_mediumType of trafficpaid_social (always)
utm_campaignThe phase bucketclient_testing / client_scaling / candidate_testing / candidate_scaling
utm_contentThe main ID — audience + trade/broad + phase + formate.g. client_scaffolders_testing_talkinghead
utm_termThe exact ad{{ad.name}} (Meta auto-fills from the ad's name)

Read it like a sentence: client → scaffolders → testing → talkinghead → this ad = "a client, from the scaffolders niche testing set, who came through a talking-head ad, ad X."

{audience}_{trade OR broad}_{phase}_{format}

  • audience → client or candidate
  • trade → scaffolders, carpenters… (or broad)
  • phase → testing or scaling
  • format → talkinghead or still
utm_content pattern
3 rules (don't break these)
  1. All lowercase, no spaces. candidate_broad_testing_talkinghead, never with capitals or spaces. UTMs are case-sensitive — capitals create duplicate rows in reports.
  2. The identifier lives in utm_content. That's the one to get right. For broad sets, use broad in place of the trade.
  3. Ad name = reporting. {{ad.name}} pulls whatever you named the ad in Meta. Name ads clearly or you'll be guessing which one pulled the lead.
Moving winners to scaling

Swap _testing → _scaling in both utm_campaign and utm_content. Format and everything else stays identical, so data stays comparable.

  2. If hacked → pause all campaigns, remove unknown users, force 2FA (see Business Manager Hacked).
  3. Have the IDs below open before you contact support or file an appeal.
  4. Loop in Les and Trung early.

2. TROUBLESHOOTING
A. Account & page health

Ad Account Disabled / Restricted

  • Symptoms: ads stop spending, "Account disabled", can't publish.
  • Immediate check: Account Quality.
  • Fix:
  1. Open Account Quality.
  2. Identify the enforcement reason.
  3. Complete Business Verification if required.
  4. Confirm 2FA for all admins.
  5. Remove unknown users.
  6. Duplicate campaigns without the flagged ads.
  7. Submit appeal.
  • Prevention: always enable 2FA, keep a backup admin, avoid aggressive claims.

Page Restricted

  • Symptoms: "Page cannot advertise".
  • Immediate check: Account Quality → Page Issues.
  • Fix:
  1. Identify the violating post/ad.
  2. Remove or edit the content.
  3. Appeal.
  4. If it's repeated, review overall page compliance.
  • Prevention: avoid misleading claims, keep a clean page history.

Ad Rejected (Policy)

  • Symptoms: rejected ads, limited delivery.
  • Immediate check: Ad → View details.
  • Fix:
  1. Read the rejection reason.
  2. Remove personal-attribute language (don't address "you" as if you know their situation — a common recruitment trip-up).
  3. Soften claims.
  4. Duplicate and resubmit.
  5. Appeal if it's wrong.
  • Prevention: use neutral recruitment wording.
B. Billing

Payment Failed

  • Symptoms: ads stop suddenly, billing errors.
  • Immediate check: Billing & Payments.
  • Fix:
  1. Check the primary card.
  2. Pay any outstanding balance.
  3. Add a backup card.
  4. Relaunch ads.
  • Prevention: always keep 2 payment methods on file.
C. Tracking

Pixel Not Firing

  • Symptoms: conversions drop to zero.
  • Immediate check: Events Manager → Diagnostics.
  • Fix:
  1. Use Test Events.
  2. Trigger the form.
  3. Confirm the event fires.
  4. Check GTM / pixel placement.
  5. Fix any duplicate firing.
  • Prevention: document the event setup, and test after any website change.

AEM / Domain Issues

  • Symptoms: iOS conversions missing.
  • Immediate check: Domains + AEM.
  • Fix:
  1. Verify the domain.
  2. Rank events in AEM.
  3. Confirm the correct pixel is attached.
  • Prevention: keep the Lead event ranked high.
D. Delivery & performance

Ads Not Spending

  • Symptoms: budget active but $0 spend.
  • Immediate check: Delivery column.
  • Fix:
  1. Check billing.
  2. Check audience size.
  3. Remove cost caps.
  4. Duplicate the ad set.
  5. Relaunch.
  • Prevention: avoid ultra-narrow targeting.

Ads Spending But Poor Leads

  • Symptoms: low-quality leads coming through.
  • Immediate check: breakdown by placement.
  • Fix:
  1. Remove low-quality placements.
  2. Add qualifying questions.
  3. Adjust copy to disqualify the wrong leads.
  4. Exclude past junk leads.
  • Prevention: add friction intentionally — you want to scare off the wrong people.

Performance Drop

  • Symptoms: CPL spikes, conversions drop.
  • Immediate check: compare last 7 days vs last 30 days.
  • Fix:
  1. Check frequency.
  2. Check CPM.
  3. Check website speed.
  4. Refresh creative first.
  5. Avoid mass edits.
  • Prevention: rotate creative every 2–3 weeks.
E. Security

Business Manager Hacked

  • Symptoms: unknown admins, sudden spend spike.
  • Immediate check: Business Settings → People.
  • Fix:
  1. Pause all campaigns.
  2. Remove unknown users.
  3. Force 2FA.
  4. Reset passwords.
  5. Contact Meta support.
  • Prevention: limit admin access to only who needs it.

IG / FB Connection Issue

  • Symptoms: can't run DM ads.
  • Immediate check: Business Settings → Accounts.
  • Fix:
  1. Reconnect Instagram.
  2. Confirm page ownership.
  3. Reassign ad account permissions.
  • Prevention: document login credentials securely (in a password manager, never in plain text).
Keeping this current

  • Whenever an ID, admin, or payment method changes → update Section 1 the same day.

Funnels & Landing Pages
What is a Funnel?

A funnel is a step-by-step online process that guides a visitor from a stranger into a lead, and ultimately into a paying customer or candidate. It's called a “funnel” because it starts wide—attracting many potential people—and narrows down to only those who take action (opt in, apply, or book a call).

A typical funnel includes:

  • A landing page with a strong hook and clear offer
  • An opt-in form to collect details (name, email, phone)
  • A thank you page with next steps (e.g. booking a call or downloading a lead magnet)
  • Automated follow-up emails to nurture that lead
Purpose of Funnels in Our Business

At Stand Up Recruitment, funnels serve a very specific purpose:

  • Generate Warm Leads (Not Cold Ones): Funnels replace outdated cold calling by attracting people already interested in what we offer—either Aussie clients who need tradies, or Kiwi tradies ready to relocate.
  • Automate Lead Generation: Instead of chasing leads, we set up funnels that bring them to us 24/7. Whether it’s a client opting in for a Risk-Free Tradie Placement info pack, or a candidate filling in a Relocation Package application, the funnel is always working.
  • Position Us as the Solution: By leading with value (like an info pack or video), we educate the lead, build trust, and naturally guide them toward taking the next step (booking a call, applying for a job, etc.).
  • Pre-Qualify the Right People: Good funnels filter out tire-kickers and bring in those who are ready to talk business or relocate. The copy, design, and flow all play a role in making sure we only speak to leads who are serious.
Why Funnels Matter (So You Never Forget)
  • Funnels scale trust — instead of you explaining the offer 10 times a day, the funnel does it for you.
  • Funnels collect data — names, emails, phones, and source of traffic, which we use to retarget or follow up.
  • Funnels save time — by letting us focus only on the leads who are already interested.
  • Funnels work 24/7 — no lunch breaks, no weekends off.

Lead Magnets
What a lead magnet is for

A lead magnet is a free, high-value thing we give away to turn a stranger into a lead. They get something useful right now; we get their details and permission to keep talking.

It's the "give" in give-give-give-then-ask. We lead with value, prove we're worth listening to, and earn the contact — instead of asking for a call before we've given them a reason to care.

What makes a good one

A lead magnet works when it:

  • Solves one narrow problem — not everything, one thing. "How to set up your Aussie bank account," not "everything about moving."
  • Is fast to consume — a checklist they read in two minutes beats a 40-page ebook nobody opens.
  • Has high perceived value — it feels like something they'd pay for.
  • Leads naturally to the next step — the free thing makes the paid/next action the obvious follow-on.

If a magnet doesn't pull the right person (a real, qualified tradie or a genuine hiring client), it's just noise. Quality of lead beats volume.

The two audiences

Lead magnets split the same way everything does — candidate vs client. They want opposite things, so they get different magnets.

  • Candidates (Kiwi tradies) want the move made easy and roles that pay. Magnets = practical tools that de-risk the move.
  • Clients (Aus employers) want proof we can supply good tradies, fast. Magnets = proof of the talent pool.

Tracking & Attribution
Why this matters

If you can't see where a lead came from, you're scaling blind. Tracking is how we tell which ad, trade, and channel actually produces leads — so we put money behind what works and cut what doesn't. Everything here feeds one question: what's bringing in the right leads, and how do we get more of them?

How leads are logged in Airtable

Airtable is the single source of truth. Every lead — from any channel — ends up here. If it's not in Airtable, it didn't happen.

  • A form submission (landing page, website, ManyChat, newsletter) fires a webhook that creates a row in Airtable automatically.
  • Each lead row captures: name, email, audience (candidate/client), trade, city, source, and the UTM data from the link they came through.
  • Instagram UTM - Instagram
  • Facebook UTM - Facebook
  • Meta Paid AD UTM - [Trade - Testing/Scaling]
  • Email UTM - Email

Rule: tag on the way in. Source and UTM data have to land with the lead, or you can't attribute it later.

UTMs + organic vs paid split

UTMs are the tags on the end of a link that tell Airtable where a click came from. They're what let us separate organic (free content, bio links) from paid (Meta ads) — the single most important split for knowing if ads are actually working.

  • Every link we put out gets tagged so its source is obvious in Airtable.
  • Standard structure: utm_source (e.g. meta, instagram, newsletter) · utm_medium (paid vs organic) · utm_campaign (the campaign) · plus content/trade where useful. [CONFIRM — SUR's exact UTM values + naming convention]
  • Paid vs organic is the headline read: paid leads should be judged on cost per lead; organic leads are "free" and prove which content to promote as paid next (prove it, then promote it).

Rule: never leave a link untagged. An untagged link becomes "direct/unknown" in Airtable and you lose the attribution.

Pixel | CAPI | GTM

This is the Meta side — how Facebook knows which ads drive results so it can optimise and we can measure them.

  • Meta Pixel — browser-side tracking. Fires when someone hits a page or submits a form.
  • CAPI (Conversions API) — server-side tracking. Sends the same events from the server, so tracking survives ad blockers and iOS privacy limits. Browser + server together = far more accurate.
  • GTM (Google Tag Manager) — the container that manages all the tags/triggers in one place, so tracking changes don't need a site rebuild.
  • Custom conversions — the specific actions we optimise toward: Submit Application (candidate) and Lead (client). [CONFIRM — current custom conversion list]
  • Event Match Quality — Meta's score for how well events match to real people. Aim for 7.0+; if it drops, tracking accuracy is suffering.

Conventions:

  • Retired conversions get an OLD - prefix (never deleted while live ad sets still reference them).
  • Test GTM in Preview mode before publishing any change.
  • Never put personal data (name, email, phone) in URL parameters.

NEW — BRIEF Trial Reels — Why We're Getting Flagged, and What Changes

For Brinda, from Les — re: the "won't get much reach — looks like something you shared before" warning on our trial reels.

What's happening

Instagram runs every Trial Reel through a duplicate/similarity check before deciding how far to push it to non-followers. When it flags a reel, reach gets suppressed immediately — sometimes down to near zero. This isn't a one-off bug: Instagram has been actively tightening this since late 2025, and it's not just checking for exact re-uploads — it's checking for pattern similarity across several signals.

Every way we can be tripping it:

  • Posting to feed first, then trialing the same clip. If a reel is already live on our grid and we then submit it (or a near copy) as a Trial, Instagram sees it as a duplicate straight away. Order matters — trial has to come before anything touches the feed, never after.
  • Re-uploading a reel that's already run as a trial. Once something's been through Trial once, running it again — even to "test it more" — reads as a repost.
  • Same underlying footage, even with a new hook, caption, cover image, or audio track swapped in. This is the one catching most people out. Instagram's detection works on the video itself, not just the metadata around it. Creators have reported getting flagged even after changing audio, caption, and slide order — because the base footage is the same. Cosmetic changes aren't enough.
  • Reusing the same audio track across multiple trial posts in a short window. Can register as a similarity signal on its own, separate from the footage.
  • Recycled or repurposed clips from other platforms (TikTok re-shares, watermarked or low-res content) — these get deprioritized on principle, flag or no flag.
  • Bulk-posting the same concept as multiple "trial" variants back to back. Even genuinely different edits, if they're clearly the same idea posted repeatedly in a short period, can read as spam/duplicate behaviour rather than legitimate testing.
What this means for how we post

The only reliable fix is genuine variance — not repackaging. A different caption or a different piece of music on the same clip does not count as a new test.

What counts:

  • Different opening 1–3 seconds shot separately (new footage, not just a new title card)
  • A different angle, setting, or delivery — not just a re-edit of the same take
  • If reworking a past winner, rebuild it as a new reel around the same idea — not a re-upload of the same file
Rule of thumb: reusing an idea is fine and encouraged. Reusing the file is what gets flagged.
The three-hook rule

Every trial batch = 3 variants of the same core reel, testing hook only. A hook is actually three layers running at once, and all three need to move together:

  • Spoken line — what's said in the first 1–3 seconds
  • On-screen text — the overlay a viewer reads
  • Visual opening — what's shown in the first frame(s)

On-screen text has to change alongside the spoken hook, not stay fixed while the voiceover changes. Around 60% of viewers watch with sound off, so for most of the audience the on-screen text is the hook — if it doesn't change across variants, we're not really testing three hooks, we're testing three voiceovers against one silent-viewer experience. It also creates a mismatch problem: if the spoken line says one thing and the text says another, that confuses the viewer in the first second and drags retention down regardless of which hook is "better."

Everything else stays identical across the three variants — same body content, same CTA, same audio bed under the voice where possible. If the body changes too, we won't know whether the hook won or the content did. The test only works if hook is the one variable.

Each variant = new spoken line + new on-screen text + new opening visual, moving as one unit, with the rest of the reel untouched.
On the testing window

Instagram evaluates over a 24–72 hour window. We've been calling winners at 24 hours, and that's fine when the gap is obvious — a clear multiple in views/completion rate between variants doesn't need the full window to be trusted.

Where it's close, give it the full 72 hours before making the call — early data can flip once the window closes, and manually sharing early cuts off the remaining evaluation, so there's no way to double back if we jump too soon.

One-line summary for the team: before posting anything to Trial — is this a reel that's already lived anywhere on our account before, and does it use footage we've already used? If yes to either, it needs a genuine reshoot of the opening or angle before it goes up — not just a new caption.

Tone of Voice

Traffic Light System

Green (lean in/celebrate/amplify)

Positive, human, and value-aligned commentary that builds trust, pride, and aspiration. Safe to engage, reshare, or highlight.

  • Success stories and milestones — comments from tradies or families who’ve made the move, sharing progress or wins.
  • Supportive community chatter — mates tagging each other, light banter, or encouragement (“@bro this is us next year”).
  • Curious or inspired reactions — people saying they’ve thought about moving or asking how it works.
  • Questions about process, trust, or logistics — “Is this legit?” “Do you help with housing?” — respond clearly and confidently.
  • Lifestyle or family-focused pride — celebrating more time with family, better pay, or quality of life.
  • UGC or lived experience — people sharing their own photos, advice, or perspectives on starting over.

Amber (case by case, careful framing):

Comments that require empathy, reassurance, or grounding before replying. Always prioritise tone and context.

  • Financial struggles or doubt — “Would love to move, but can’t afford it.” Frame around support and planning, not persuasion.
  • Family hesitation — “My partner’s not keen.” / “Kids wouldn’t want to move.” Approach with empathy and no judgment.
  • Job dissatisfaction or frustration — “Bosses here don’t care.” / “No good jobs left.” Acknowledge the reality, steer toward hope.
  • Mild sarcasm or off-topic humour — “Yeah right, when pigs fly.” Light humour back is fine if it keeps tone friendly.
  • Constructive scepticism — “Seems risky.” “What’s the catch?” Respond once with calm, factual reassurance.
  • Cultural nuance — comments about leaving home, missing NZ, or adjusting to Aussie life. Keep tone grounded and balanced.
  • UGC from influencers or third parties — ensure authenticity before engaging. Avoid overhyping or salesy tone.

Red (avoid / don’t engage):

Commentary that risks trust, fuels negativity, or derails conversation. Avoid public engagement; escalate or hide if needed.

  • Political or polarising debates — migration policy, cost of living blame, government criticism.
  • Hostile or defamatory comments — accusations of scamming, exploiting workers, or unethical practice.
  • Negative comparisons — “Aussies are better than Kiwis.” / “NZ is rubbish.” — don’t validate divisive commentary.
  • Inflammatory or discriminatory language — racism, sexism, or derogatory comments about trades, nationalities, or families.
  • Sensitive disclosures — mental health, personal crisis, or trauma. Acknowledge gently, then move offline via DM if appropriate.
  • Trolling or baiting — repeated sarcastic or aggressive comments. Don’t feed; hide, block, or escalate.
  • Overly corporate or defensive replies — avoid sounding like PR; if clarification is needed, respond once with humanity and clarity.

Problem 1: How to contact them

1. Software

  • Subscribe to as many lead tools as you can (names, job titles, contact info).
  • Try 100 leads from each.
  • Keep the software that gives the most up-to-date, responsive leads.

2. Brokers (paid)

  • Go to several brokers based on your audience criteria.
  • Get a sample from each.
  • Test the samples.
  • Stick with the brokers whose lists actually convert.

3. Manual

  • Join groups and communities where your target audience hangs out.
  • Does the group link to social profiles?
  • Yes → add to list.
  • No → reach out directly.
  • Don't come off as transactional or opportunistic.
  • Trade-off: good — most companies haven't found these people yet. Bad — it's slow.
  • Search "outbound lead scraping tool" or "database lead scraping" to find tools + brokers.
  • Interest groups are gold — they're already looking for a solution (and there's software for this).
Problem 2: What to say to them

1. Personalisation

  • Compliment their work — target people who already like what they do.
  • Know something real about the person; make it feel like warm outreach.
  • Emails: personal subject lines. They'll appreciate the effort you took.
  • Research each lead first — do it yourself, pay someone, or use software.
  • Tip: write at a 3rd-grade reading level → ~50% more responses. Run it through a free reading-level checker.

2. Big fast value

  • Blow their mind in under 30 seconds.
  • Run your first ~100 conversations (or ~10,000 emails), then tweak.

Newsletter

This section is built for candidate newsletters: emails we send to keep our Kiwi tradies warm, engaged, and moving forward.

Our goal is simple: bombard them with value. Every email should give something real tools, insights, checklists, stories, playbooks or small wins they can use right now.

Each newsletter should:

  • Educate: Teach them something that helps them land work or prepare for the move.
  • Equip: Give them a tangible tool, script, or resource they can save or use that day.
  • Engage: Keep the tone human, grounded, and real, like Les talking to a mate.
  • Encourage: Remind them why they started, and that the opportunity is waiting across the ditch.

The mission: Build trust.

We stay top of mind by overdelivering on value, week after week, so when they’re ready to move, there’s only one name they think of: Stand Up Recruitment.

The difference with this newsletter is simple, it’s not coming from a company. It’s coming straight from Les, to build his personal brand.

Structure

Frequency:

  • Send every 2 weeks (Friday mornings NZ time).
  • Extra sends are fine for major updates (e.g. new roles, christmas/new years, webinars).

Newsletter Flow (Every Send):

  1. Hook / Subject Line
  • Short, curiosity-driven, or value-focused.
  • Feels conversational — “Les talking to a mate.”
  1. Story / Insight (Middle Section)
  • Real example, short story, or lesson from Les.
  • Proves credibility, shows personality, or shares a quick win.
  1. Value Section (Main takeaway)
  • Give something real: checklist, tool, tip, prompt, or short how-to.
  • “Bombard them with value” — something they can use today.
  1. Soft CTA (End)
  • Always one action — reply keyword or simple button.
  • CTAs should feel like help, not a sale.
  • Examples:
  • “Click here for your free CV template.”
  • “Want the downloadable version? Reply and I’ll send it through.”
