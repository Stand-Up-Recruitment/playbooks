Recruiter Ramp

Purpose: get a new recruiter capable of running the full placement process solo — sourcing, reference checks, candidate meetings, and client interviews — within four weeks of starting.

Success metric: by the end of week 4, the trainee can run the full process solo, start to finish. The target is capability, not a closed placement — a placement may still be pending at week 4 depending on job flow, and that's expected.

Four-Week Timeline
When Focus Checkpoint
Days 1–3 Interactive playbook: read / watch / do. Includes recorded videos of reference checks and sourcing calls. End of Day 3 — verbal walkthrough: trainee explains the reference check and sourcing process back, cold, before touching a live candidate.
Days 3–5 First reps: own sourcing calls and reference checks on real candidates, scripts in hand. Recruiters correct live in-room. Roles

  • Les — runs day-to-day of the ramp: check-ins, debriefs, checkpoint sign-off.

  • Recruiters — correct in the moment when the trainee is doing something wrong, since training happens in the same room. Trainees shadow whichever recruiter has a client-candidate interview or candidate/team meeting running — whoever is available, without taking anything off their plate.

  • Trainee — works through the playbook, then moves through sourcing, reference checks, and meetings at the pace the checkpoints allow.

  • Recorded videos of reference checks stand in for live shadowing before a trainee's first solo attempt — the video is the model.

Stage 3

Screening Call

Tell: What You Need to Know

The screening call is a short call to check whether a candidate is right for the job, and ready to move soon enough to book. You're checking the basics: their trade, their experience, their qualifications, when they can move, and whether they're a NZ citizen. It's not a deep interview. That's the internal interview.

Every screening call ends one of three ways:

  1. Qualified and can move within 3 months: book the internal interview before you hang up

  2. Qualified but can't move within 3 months: update their details and move on. The folders will sort them.

  3. Not suitable: close the call politely and update their status

After every call where you speak to the candidate, you update the four required fields. The readiness folders and your daily alert email are built from them.

Full Breakdown
Before the call

  • Have the candidate's JobAdder profile open

  • Read their CV. Know their trade and experience before you dial.

  • Have the job brief open, so you know the must-haves

  • Check for client feedback on this job. If a previous candidate failed the client interview, add those points to your questions.

Making the call

You don't dial candidates yourself. A bot connects the call, so it can be recorded and transcribed automatically.

  1. Open the candidate's profile in JobAdder

  2. Hover over the status section below the candidate's name

  3. Select Screening

  4. Your phone will ring. Pick it up and press any key.

  5. The bot then calls the candidate and connects you

Because the call is recorded and transcribed, you don't need to take notes. Focus on the conversation.

The script

Opening: "Hey [Name]! It's [Your Name] from Stand Up Recruitment. You showed interest in work in Australia, is that right?"

  1. Why move: "What's making you want to make the move to Australia?"

  2. Skills and experience: "I can see that you are a [trade]. Can you expand a bit more on your skills and experience?"

  3. Qualifications: "Are you qualified in that trade?" / "Do you hold a current [specific ticket]?"

  4. Citizenship: "And are you a New Zealand citizen?"

  5. When they can move: "When are you looking to make the move?"

  6. Where: "Whereabouts in Australia are you keen to go?"

  7. Pay: "And what sort of pay are you after?"

  8. Anything to add: "Great! Is there anything else you'd like us to know?"

Questions 5, 6 and 7 give you three of your four required fields. Never skip them.

Close: qualified and can move within 3 months

"Sweet! It sounds like you'd be a really good fit for what we've got on at the moment. The next step is a proper chat where I'll run you through the role in detail, the pay, where it's based and how the move over works, and answer any questions you've got. It takes about 15 to 20 minutes."

If they're in Auckland: "You're welcome to pop into our office for it, or we can do it on a Teams video call. Which suits you?"

If they're outside Auckland: "It's a video call on Teams."

"Are you free [today / tomorrow] at [time]?"

Lock in a time before you hang up, then:

"Perfect, I've booked you in for [day] at [time]. You'll get an email with the details shortly, and a text reminder on the morning. Have a think about any questions you've got about the move. Talk soon!"

Close: qualified but can't move within 3 months

Don't treat this as a dead end. We want them to come back to us when they're ready, not apply for a job in Australia on their own.

"No worries at all, that's really common. Just so you know, when you're ready we can have a job sorted before you even leave New Zealand, with free temporary accommodation and a free flight over. I'll touch base closer to [month]. If anything changes before then, just give me a call."

How to Use This Guide
The ethos behind every script in this guide: every interaction - whether it's a candidate, a referee, or a client - is a conversation, not a form you're working through. Build rapport. Be genuinely curious about the person in front of you. Ask follow-up questions because you actually want to know the answer, not because a script told you to. The words matter less than the mindset you bring into them.
S6 Screening Call

For sourcers. Read the tone notes, not just the words. You don't dial candidates yourself: set their status to Screening in JobAdder and the bot connects the call, so it's recorded and transcribed. That means no notes to take, so give the candidate your full attention.

1. The Open - Confirm Interest, Not Interrogate
Sound like you're following up, not cold calling.

This isn't a cold call and it shouldn't feel like one. The candidate already raised their hand - they showed interest somewhere along the line. Your opening line is just confirming that, warmly, like you're following up on something they started, not starting something yourself.

You: Hey [Name]! (pause) It's [Your Name] from Stand Up Recruitment. (pause) You showed interest in work in Australia, is that right?

Pause after their name and after you've said who you are. These two pauses give them a beat to register that this is a real person on the line and place who you are, before you ask them anything. Skip the pauses and the whole line runs together - they're still processing "who is this" while you've already moved on to the question.

Candidate: Yeah, that's right.

Say it like you already know the answer is yes. If they sound confused or say no, don't panic - just clarify where the interest came from (an ad, a database entry, a referral) and reset from there.

2. The Why - Get Them Talking About Themselves
Don't accept the first answer - dig one layer deeper.

This is the most important question in the whole call, and it's also the one recruiters rush past the most. Don't ask it and move on the second you get an answer. A generic answer like "better money" or "just want a change" is the start of the conversation, not the end of it.

You: What's making you want to make the move to Australia?

Let them talk. Don't jump in with the next question the second they pause - that pause is often them thinking of more to say. If they give you something surface-level, gently dig one layer deeper: "Yeah? What's driving that for you at the moment?" or "Is there something specific behind that?" You're not interrogating them, you're just genuinely curious. People can tell the difference, and it's the difference between a transactional call and one where they actually open up.

3. Skills and Experience - Let Them Sell Themselves
Show them you've paid attention before you ask anything.

Frame this as recognition, not a request. "I can see that you are a [trade]" tells them you've already looked at their profile - it shows you're paying attention, not running through a checklist.

You: I can see that you are a [trade]. Can you expand a bit more on your skills and experience?

Let them run with this. If they're vague or give you a one-liner, prompt gently - "What kind of projects were you working on day to day?" or "What's been the biggest job you've handled recently?" Once they've given you a general picture, that's your cue to ask the specific qualification questions for the role you're screening for.

4. Readiness to Move - Anchor the Timeline
Acknowledge before you move on - don't just log it and go.

This is a practical question but it still needs warmth. You're not checking a box, you're trying to understand where they're actually at in their head - some people have already made peace with moving, others are still testing the idea out loud for the first time.

You: And when are you looking to make the move?

Acknowledge whatever they say before moving on - don't just note it down and jump to the next question. A simple "Okay, that's good to know" or "Right, that gives us something to work with" keeps it feeling like a conversation rather than data collection.

Where and Pay: Fill Your Required Fields
Two quick questions you can never skip.

The readiness folders and daily alerts are built from the candidate's Notice Period, Location and Ideal Salary. You just got the first one. These two get you the rest. Ask them naturally, straight off the back of the timeline question.

You: Whereabouts in Australia are you keen to go?
You: And what sort of pay are you after?

If they say "anywhere", ask which state they'd pick first. If they're unsure on pay, ask what they're on now and what would make the move worth it. Either way, you want something you can put in the field.

Sub-Script: If They Can't Move Within 3 Months
This is not a dead end. It's a future booking you're locking in right now.

If the candidate says any version of "not looking to go for 3 months, 6 months, next year" - this is where you use it. The whole point is to make sure that when they are ready, they come back through Stand Up Recruitment instead of quietly self-applying for a job in Australia somewhere else. You do this by making the Grand Slam offer sound too good to walk away from - because it is. Why would they not want a job sorted before they even leave New Zealand? Why would they not want free temporary accommodation? Why would they not want a free flight? That's your job to make them realise.

1. Build Rapport (New)

You: Hey, did you know we're actually based here in Auckland, Takapuna?

Candidates often assume they're talking to an overseas call centre. A quick, genuine local detail like this builds trust fast, before you even get to the offer. Drop it in naturally wherever it fits in the conversation - it doesn't need to be the very first line.

2. Acknowledge

You: Hey, yeah, no worries - a lot of people we talk to aren't ready right now.

3. Reiterate the Grand Slam Offer

You: But the reason people do end up coming back through us, [Name], is because we sort you out with a job offer before you even leave New Zealand. That's going to save you a lot of stress, time, and wasted money when you get over there - because otherwise you're waiting on callbacks, going for interviews, it's just extra stress you don't want, right?

(Pause. Get the micro-yes.)

You: On top of that, most of our clients offer temporary free accommodation if you need it. That lets you save up a bit before you get sorted. If your family's moving over too, depending on the case they can come after you, once you're settled. And if you've already got somewhere to stay, that's all good too. On top of that, we'll sort your free flight over as well.

(Pause. Get the yes on each point before moving on.)

These pauses matter more than they look on the page. The first one gets a small agreement before you build on it further - don't rush past it into the next sentence. Then take each point of the offer at its own pace rather than reading it as one block - a small yes on accommodation, a small yes on family, a small yes on the flight. If you fill the silence yourself, you're answering for them instead of letting them commit.

4. The Capture Question (Updated)

You: So knowing all that, [Name], is there any reason you wouldn't come back through us when it's time to move to Australia?

This replaces the old close question. Les's preferred phrasing on purpose - "is there any reason you wouldn't" surfaces any hidden objection now instead of it showing up as a no-show later. If they say something like "well, my uncle owns a business over there and can get me a job," that's fine - still say "perfect" and move on to lock a date anyway. We still want them captured.

Alternative phrasing if it fits the conversation better: "So knowing all that, are you going to give me a call back when it's time to move to Australia?"

(Pause. Let them answer either way.)

5. Lock a Rough Date

You: Great, so roughly what month are we talking here?

Get a real month - not "sometime next year." A vague timeframe gives them no reason to actually call back, and gives you nothing to nurture them against later.

6. Confirm and Set Expectation

You: Yep, sweet, I'll pencil that in and give you a call back as we get closer. I'm gonna send you over a text message with my details, so if anything changes, you can get a hold of me before that. All good?

Wait for a verbal yes before ending the call - don't just assume it and move on.

7. Close

You: Perfect, talk soon [Name].

This is a scaffold, not a script to recite word for word. Use what works, drop what doesn't, and make it sound like you. After the call, update the four required fields (Status, Notice Period, Location and Ideal Salary) and send the Not Ready Yet follow-up text. The folders sort the candidate from there. See “What happened on the call?” in the Sourcer Playbook.

5. Trade-Specific Qualifications - Confirm the Non-Negotiables
The one part that's closer to a checklist - keep the energy up anyway.

This is the one part of the call that is genuinely closer to a checklist, and that's fine - the candidate expects some practical questions. Just don't let the tone shift into interrogation mode. Keep the same conversational energy you've had the whole call.

You: Are you qualified in that trade? Do you hold a current [specific ticket]?
You: And are you a New Zealand citizen?

Citizenship is a hard requirement. Ask it plainly and move on. If they're not a citizen, close the call politely using the not suitable close.

Ask the specific tickets or qualifications relevant to the role brief. Acknowledge their answers before moving on - even a quick "Perfect, that's exactly what we need" keeps the energy up.

6. Anything to Add - Leave the Door Open
Don't skip this - the best information often shows up here.

This question often gets skipped because it feels like filler, but it's not. It gives the candidate a chance to raise something you didn't think to ask about, and it signals that you're interested in them as a whole person, not just the boxes you needed ticked.

You: Great! Is there anything else you would like us to know or anything you would like to add?

Whatever they say here, take it seriously - sometimes the most useful information in the whole call comes out in this moment because the pressure of "being asked questions" has lifted.

7. The Close: Qualified and Can Move Within 3 Months
Sell the next step, then book them in with the placer before you hang up.

A lot of recruiters jump straight to "can we book a call" without giving the candidate a reason to say yes. Give them a hook first: who they'll talk to and what they'll get out of it. Then lock in a real day and time. Never end with a vague "we'll be in touch."

You: Sweet! It sounds like you'd be a really good fit for what we've got on at the moment. The next step is a proper chat with [Placer Name], one of our recruiters. (pause) They'll run you through the role in detail, the pay, where it's based and how the move over works, and they can answer any questions you've got. It takes about 15 to 20 minutes.

Pause after naming the placer. It lets the candidate register that they're being handed to a real person for a real next step.

You: (If they're in Auckland) You're welcome to pop into our office for it, or we can do it on a Teams video call. Which suits you?
You: Are you free [today / tomorrow] at [time]?
You: Perfect, I've booked you in with [Placer Name] for [day] at [time]. You'll get an email with the details shortly, and a text reminder on the morning. Have a think about any questions you've got about the move. Talk soon!

Only book candidates who can move within 3 months (Ready to go or Hot List). Aim for same day or next day, and book it in JobAdder before you move to your next call. See “Who do I book for?” in the Sourcer Playbook.

8. The Close - Candidate Is Not Suitable
This is their last impression of us - make it a good one.

Say it slow and stay warm. This person gave you their time and their story - the way you close the call is the last impression they have of Stand Up Recruitment, so make it a good one.

You: Awesome, thanks so much for chatting with me today [Name]. We will be in touch if anything comes up that matches your experience. Have a good one!

How to Use the Tailored Interview Guide Without Sounding Like a Checklist
The guide is a reference document, not a script order.

Before you get into the actual flow of this interview, it's worth understanding how to use the Tailored Interview Guide properly. When recruiters first started using it, the most common feedback was that it felt overwhelming - a whole document full of questions can easily start to feel like a form you're working through rather than a conversation. Here's how to use it so that doesn't happen.

1. Never read the questions in the order they're generated. The guide gives you a Client Intent Summary plus three categories of questions - Commitment, Role and Location, and Technical. That's a reference for you to draw from, not a script to read top to bottom. Read the whole thing before the call, understand why each question is there, then weave the questions in naturally wherever they actually fit based on what the candidate is saying.

2. Anchor questions to something the candidate just said. This is the same technique that runs through every script in this guide - instead of announcing the next question on your list, connect it to what they just told you. For example, instead of "Next question: are you comfortable with the remote location?" try "You mentioned you grew up rurally - how do you think that'll translate to a site like this one, pretty out of the way?" Same information, completely different feel.

3. Use the Client Intent Summary as context, not something you announce. Never say out loud "the client mentioned reliability concerns so I need to ask you about commitment." That's transparently box-ticking and the candidate will feel it. The summary should only inform which questions you reach for and how you frame them - it stays invisible to the candidate.

4. Only ask what you don't already know. If Part 0 - Uncover the Real Why already revealed genuine motivation and commitment, you don't need to mechanically work through every commitment question on the list. Adapt based on what's already surfaced. The guide is there to make sure nothing important gets missed, not to force you through every line regardless of what's already been covered.

If you're using more than one Tailored Interview Guide in the same call because the candidate could fit multiple jobs, this matters even more - you're holding two or three documents worth of questions in your head at once, and the conversation will only feel natural if you're pulling from them selectively rather than working through them like a checklist.

You: Hey, did you know we're actually based here in Auckland, Takapuna?

Candidates often assume they're talking to an overseas call centre. A quick, genuine local detail like this builds trust fast, before you even get to the offer. Drop it in naturally wherever it fits in the conversation - it doesn't need to be the very first line.

2. Acknowledge

You: Hey, yeah, no worries - a lot of people we talk to aren't ready right now.

3. Reiterate the Grand Slam Offer

You: But the reason people do end up coming back through us, [Name], is because we sort you out with a job offer before you even leave New Zealand. That's going to save you a lot of stress, time, and wasted money when you get over there - because otherwise you're waiting on callbacks, going for interviews, it's just extra stress you don't want, right?

(Pause. Get the micro-yes.)

You: On top of that, most of our clients offer temporary free accommodation if you need it. That lets you save up a bit before you get sorted. If your family's moving over too, depending on the case they can come after you, once you're settled. And if you've already got somewhere to stay, that's all good too. On top of that, we'll sort your free flight over as well.

(Pause. Get the yes on each point before moving on.)

Let each of these pauses do their job. The first gets a small agreement to build on. Then take each point of the offer at its own pace - a small yes on accommodation, a small yes on family, a small yes on the flight - rather than reading it as one block.

4. The Capture Question (Updated)

You: So knowing all that, [Name], is there any reason you wouldn't come back through us when it's time to move to Australia?

This replaces the old close question. Les's preferred phrasing on purpose - "is there any reason you wouldn't" surfaces any hidden objection now instead of it showing up as a no-show later. If they say something like "well, my uncle owns a business over there and can get me a job," that's fine - still say "perfect" and move on to lock a date anyway. We still want them captured.

Alternative phrasing if it fits the conversation better: "So knowing all that, are you going to give me a call back when it's time to move to Australia?"

(Pause. Let them answer either way.)

5. Lock a Rough Date

You: Great, so roughly what month are we talking here?

Get a real month, not "sometime next year." A vague timeframe gives them no reason to call back and gives you nothing to nurture them against.

6. Confirm and Set Expectation

You: Yep, sweet, I'll pencil that in and give you a call back as we get closer. I'm gonna send you over a text message with my details, so if anything changes, you can get a hold of me before that. All good?

You: Perfect, talk soon [Name].

After the call, update their Notice Period with the month they gave you and send the Not Ready Yet follow-up text. The folders sort them from there.

4. Financial Readiness Discussion
This exists to protect the candidate, not to interrogate them. Frame it that way.

This conversation exists to reduce dropouts after the offer - but if you frame it as "we need to check you can afford this," it feels transactional and a bit cold. Frame it instead as looking out for them: you'd rather they know the real numbers now than get blindsided later. That framing changes everything about how this lands.

Walk the candidate through the real costs - flights, accommodation, and a car, landing at roughly $5,000 all up to be comfortable.

You: Moving over costs more than most people expect. Your flight alone is $500 to $1,000. Accommodation runs $350 to $850 a week. Most people need a car within the first few weeks. So realistically, before your first pay comes in, you're looking at around $5,000 all up. (pause) Does that align with where you're at financially?

Pause before asking if it aligns. You've just given them a genuinely confronting number - rushing straight into the question doesn't give them a chance to actually process it. Let it sit for a second.

Path 1 - Clearly ready: "Perfect. We also offer a relocation package if you want it handled for you - I'll send you the info. Totally optional. Right, let's keep going..."

Path 2 - Unsure: "I appreciate you being straight with me. Use the cool off period to sit down and work out the numbers - I'll send you a cost guide."

Path 3 - Clearly not ready: "Thanks for being honest. If the finances aren't there right now it's going to be tough to make this work. Let's pause, you get yourself in a better position, and we reconnect when the timing is right."

Whichever path they land on, thank them for being straight with you. Candidates who are honest about money now are the ones who don't fall over later - reward that honesty with warmth, not disappointment.

5. Work History and Technical Assessment
Let them talk about their actual work - this is where their real expertise comes through.

This is often where candidates relax the most, because they're talking about something they know well and are proud of. Let them run with it, and use this as your opening to weave in the Commitment Questions, Role and Location Questions, and Technical Questions from your Tailored Interview Guide - going deeper rather than firing them one after another like a quiz.

You: Talk me through your experience - what have you been working on recently? Walk me through the last role or two.

Listen carefully, ask clarifying questions, and note any gaps against the job brief must-haves. As they talk, look for natural openings to bring in your Commitment and Role and Location Questions if you haven't covered them yet - then move into the 3 Technical Questions from your Tailored Interview Guide.

You: What's the thing that's going to keep you going on the tough days out there?
6. Close
Wrap up on a high note, but make sure nothing gets left hanging.

There are three things to cover here and none of them can be skipped - the cool off period, referees, and police check details. Do this in one clean sweep at the end rather than scattering it through the call. End the call with the same energy you opened it with - this is exciting for them, don't let the admin at the end flatten that.

Cool off period:

You: Before we move you to the client interview stage, I want you to take the next 2 days to properly think this through. Relocating to Australia is a big decision. (pause) I'll send you an email with a summary of the role and the relocation information. We'll touch base in 2 days and decide together whether to move forward.

Pause after acknowledging it's a big decision before moving into the practical next steps - let the weight of the decision land before you talk logistics.

Referees: Ask for 2 previous supervisors - name, company, and contact number for each. Stay on the call to collect these. If the candidate says they'll send them later, let them know the details need to come directly to you.

Police check details: Let the candidate know you'll need their full name, email, date of birth, gender, contact number, and residential address.

Do not progress the candidate during the cool off period. Wait for the 2-day follow-up call.

P6 Client Interview

For placers. Read the tone notes, not just the words. This one's different from the others. You're not the one being interviewed and you're not the one interviewing - you're in the room to make sure the conversation between two other people goes well. That's a different skill. Most of this script is about presence and timing, not lines to recite.

1. The Open - Introduce, Then Get Out of the Way
Set the tone in one breath, then step back.

Your job in the opening is to make the introduction feel natural and then hand the floor over. Don't linger here - the longer you talk, the less this feels like the candidate and client's conversation.

You: [Client name], this is [Candidate first name]. [Candidate], this is [Client name] from [Company]. (pause) I'll let you two get into it - I'm just here to help the conversation along if needed.

Pause briefly after the introductions before handing it over - it gives both sides a beat to actually look at each other and register who's who, rather than being talked over into the next thing.

After the introduction, hand over the direction of the interview based on whether this is a new or existing client.

If it's a new client:

You: [Client name], how would you like to run this - would you want to tell [Candidate] a bit more about the company first, or would you prefer [Candidate] walks you through his experience to start?

New clients haven't worked with us before, so giving them the choice up front shows respect for how they like to operate and sets a collaborative tone from the very first minute.

If it's an existing client:

You: [Candidate], give [Client] a quick rundown on your skills and experience.

With an existing client, you already know their style and they already trust the process - you can move straight into the candidate's rundown without needing to check in on format first.

2. Reading the Room - When to Step In for the Candidate
Watch for the moment they need a lifeline, not the moment they need rescuing.

There's a difference between a candidate who's thinking before they answer and a candidate who's actually stuck. Give them the space to work through it first - jumping in too early undermines them in front of the client. Step in only when it's clear they need help getting the good stuff out.

You: [Candidate], you mentioned to me that you can do [x, y, z] - can you walk [Client] through how you approach that on site?

Frame it as prompting a memory, not correcting a wrong answer. This lets the candidate save face and still land the point.

3. Reading the Room - When to Step In for the Client
Sometimes the client needs a nudge to ask the thing they actually want to know.

Occasionally the client will circle around a concern without directly asking about it - commitment, reliability, whatever's on their mind. If you can tell there's an unspoken question sitting in the room, you can surface it for them.

You: [Candidate], [Client] is looking for someone who will commit to at least 24 months in this role - is that something you see yourself doing?

Only do this if it genuinely helps move things toward a yes. You're not creating problems that weren't there - you're clearing up ones that already exist so they can be addressed directly instead of lingering unspoken.

Setting the real stakes - say this in front of everyone:

This is a different kind of moment - you're deliberately putting a serious consequence on the table, in front of both the candidate and the client, so nobody can say later they weren't told. It also doubles as a genuine confidence check on the candidate. Slow this one right down.

You: Just one more thing while all the parties are here - [Candidate], if you get over there and you can't do what you've told us you can do, I just want to be completely clear that the client is not going to keep you around or offer you a job. (pause) Are you confident with your skills and the things that we talked about? (pause)

Pause after stating the consequence, before asking about their confidence, and then again after asking it. The first pause lets the seriousness of "not keeping you around" actually land. The second gives them real space to answer honestly rather than reflexively saying yes just to keep the momentum going. Don't run all three into one breath. This isn't about scaring the candidate - it's about making sure their yes is a real yes.

Once the candidate and client are both aligned and confident, close the loop for everyone in the room:

You: With that being said, is there any other questions from either parties?

This is a small line but it matters - it signals the conversation is wrapping up on solid ground, and it gives both sides one last genuine chance to raise anything before you move to the debrief. Don't skip it just because things feel settled.

4. The Debrief - Release the Candidate Gracefully
Get them off the call warmly and without lingering.

This moment can feel a bit awkward if you let it drag. Keep it short, warm, and clear about what happens next.

You: Thanks [Candidate] - we'll be in touch very soon. I'm just going to have a quick chat with [Client] now.
5. The Debrief - Get the Real Answer from the Client
Ask it directly. Don't hedge, and don't fill the space while they think.

Once the candidate's off the call, get straight to it. Clients appreciate directness here more than a soft lead-in - you've both just spent time on this, they know what you're about to ask.

You: What did you think? Does [Candidate] tick the boxes for you?

If they pause before answering, let them. They're forming a genuine opinion, not stalling.

6. If the Answer Is No - Extract Real Feedback
Vague feedback helps nobody. Push gently for something specific.

"Not quite right" or "just not the one" isn't useful - it doesn't tell you anything to adjust for the next candidate. Ask for something concrete, and frame it as helping you help them, not as defending your candidate.

You: Just so I can improve the next batch - what was missing for you?

Whatever they give you, log it properly in JobAdder. This is the difference between your next submission being a guess and being genuinely targeted.

7. Always Leave With a Locked-In Date
Every single debrief ends with a specific date - no exceptions.

This is non-negotiable, whether the answer was yes or no, whether they need to check with someone else or just need a day to think. "I'll get back to you" is not an answer you accept and move on from.

You: No worries - when can we expect a decision by?

Once you have that date, it's live. If it passes without word, that's what triggers the Contract Follow-Up cadence (P8 in the Placer Playbook) - so getting a real, specific date here isn't just good manners, it's what makes the whole follow-up system work.

Job Setup

Tell: What You Need to Know

There are four steps: take the job, understand it, post the job ad, and create a task for your sourcer. Understanding the job should take 10 to 15 minutes. Don't rush it. If you don't understand the job, you can't judge whether a candidate is right for it.

Full Breakdown
Step 1: Take the job and assign your workflow

New jobs are emailed to recruiters when sales closes a client. When one comes in for your trades:

  1. Go to the JobAdder home page

  2. Find the new job under the No Applications stage

  3. Click the job to open it

  4. Go to the Details tab and click Edit

  5. Under Workflow, select your own workflow

  6. Click Save

Important: jobs come through without a workflow, so you have to assign it every time. If you skip this, the job won't track against you.

Step 2: Understand the job (10 to 15 minutes)

  1. Read the job description on the job card

  2. Go to Airtable and download the sales call transcript for the job

  3. Open Claude and run the SUR Tailored Interview Guide Generator prompt with the job description and the transcript. Save the output, because you'll use it in every internal interview for this job.

  4. Read the interview guide, especially the Client Intent Summary. It picks up things the client said on the sales call that aren't in the job description.

  5. Check that the job description, the transcript and the interview guide all match, especially on:

    • Pay rate and pay structure (hourly or salary, overtime, super)

    • Hours per week

    • Location

    • Accommodation, vehicle or other benefits

    • Must-have qualifications and experience

If anything doesn't match, especially pay, raise it with the sales team before the job goes to your sourcer.

Step 3: Create and post the job ad

  1. Open your Claude job ad thread

  2. Paste in the SUR Job Ad Prompt for Aus with the details from the job description

  3. Read the ad before posting. Never post anything you haven't checked.

  4. Name the ad Role | State, for example Electrician | Queensland

  5. In JobAdder: Actions → Post Job → paste the ad → select the platforms → Post

Trade Me If needed

Check each platform to make sure the ad is showing correctly.

Step 4: Create a task for your sourcer

This is how your sourcer finds out about the new job.

  1. Click the job card

  2. In the top right corner, click the Action drop-down and select Create Task

  3. Add a task title, for example Review job

  4. Set the task date and priority

  5. Under Assignee, select your sourcer

  6. In the Activity drop-down, select Take Job Brief

  7. Click Create

Your sourcer gets a notification on the bell icon in the bottom left corner of JobAdder. They'll start calling from the next morning.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Forgetting to assign your workflow

  • Skimming the transcript, or skipping the interview guide

  • Creating the task for your sourcer when the pay doesn't match across the documents

  • Posting the job ad without reading it

  • Forgetting to create the task, or assigning it to the wrong sourcer

Internal Interview

The internal interview is the most important conversation you have with a candidate before they meet the client. Get this right and everything after runs smoothly.

Tell: What You Need to Know

Your sourcer has already screened every candidate they book with you, so you don't need to re-check the basics. You go straight into selling the opportunity and digging deeper.

The interview is on Teams, or in person at our office if the candidate is in Auckland. The process is the same either way. Always use the interview guide you made for the job in P1. Never wing this one.

Opening

"Hey [Name], good to meet you properly. Thanks for making the time. This is just a chat so I can give you a proper rundown of the role and see if it's a good fit for where you're at."

Part 0: Uncover the real why

This is the first real question in every interview. Les is firm on this: a surface answer is not good enough.

"What's making you want to make the move to Australia?"

Most candidates start with a surface answer, like better money or better weather. Ask 2 to 3 follow-up questions until you get the real reason:

  • "Sure, but what would that extra money actually let you do that you can't do right now?"

  • "Is there something specific driving that? A house deposit, supporting family back home, paying off debt?"

  • "Why does that matter to you at this point in your life?"

The real reason tells you whether they're genuinely motivated, and gives you something to come back to when you sell the opportunity and during the cool off.

Part 1: Run through the opportunity

Use the selling points from the job description. Make it exciting and relevant to this candidate. Cover the company, the duties, the pay, the type of work, growth, and lifestyle in Australia.

If accommodation or a vehicle is part of the offer, know whether it's temporary or permanent, how long it lasts, and what the candidate pays before you mention it. Don't oversell it. It comes back to bite you at the offer stage.

"From what you've told me, I think this could be a really good fit for you. It's not just a job, it's a genuine opportunity to step up, earn well, and set yourself up properly."

Part 2: Check they're keen

"Does that sound like something you'd want to hear more about?"

If they're hesitant, find out why before you go on.

Part 3: Screening questions

  • "If you were to move, what's your timeframe?"

  • "Would you be moving over on your own, or bringing family with you?"

  • "We don't need to know your criminal history, but is there anything serious that could stop you from moving to Australia for work?"

  • "Any medical conditions or restrictions we should be aware of that might affect site work?"

  • "Do you have a valid passport with at least 6 months validity?"

  • "Do you have any planned holidays or commitments in the next few months?"

If anything rules them out, close the call politely. If their timeframe has changed, update their Notice Period after the call. The folders will sort them.

The Day 2 follow-up call

This is a real checkpoint, not a formality. Cover:

  • Financial readiness, with actual numbers

  • Whether they're happy to start within 4 to 6 weeks

  • Whether they're still happy with the location

  • Whether they've genuinely talked it through with their partner and family

  • Whether this move makes sense for where they want to be in a few years

Then:

  • Hesitant: give them 2 more days. Don't push for a decision on this call.

How to Use This Guide
The ethos behind every script in this guide: every interaction - whether it's a candidate, a referee, or a client - is a conversation, not a form you're working through. Build rapport. Be genuinely curious about the person in front of you. Ask follow-up questions because you actually want to know the answer, not because a script told you to. The words matter less than the mindset you bring into them.

How to Use the Tailored Interview Guide Without Sounding Like a Checklist
The guide is a reference document, not a script order.

Before you get into the actual flow of this interview, it's worth understanding how to use the Tailored Interview Guide properly. When recruiters first started using it, the most common feedback was that it felt overwhelming - a whole document full of questions can easily start to feel like a form you're working through rather than a conversation. Here's how to use it so that doesn't happen.

1. Never read the questions in the order they're generated. The guide gives you a Client Intent Summary plus three categories of questions - Commitment, Role and Location, and Technical. That's a reference for you to draw from, not a script to read top to bottom. Read the whole thing before the call, understand why each question is there, then weave the questions in naturally wherever they actually fit based on what the candidate is saying.

2. Anchor questions to something the candidate just said. This is the same technique that runs through every script in this guide - instead of announcing the next question on your list, connect it to what they just told you. For example, instead of "Next question: are you comfortable with the remote location?" try "You mentioned you grew up rurally - how do you think that'll translate to a site like this one, pretty out of the way?" Same information, completely different feel.

3. Use the Client Intent Summary as context, not something you announce. Never say out loud "the client mentioned reliability concerns so I need to ask you about commitment." That's transparently box-ticking and the candidate will feel it. The summary should only inform which questions you reach for and how you frame them - it stays invisible to the candidate.

4. Only ask what you don't already know. If Part 0 - Uncover the Real Why already revealed genuine motivation and commitment, you don't need to mechanically work through every commitment question on the list. Adapt based on what's already surfaced. The guide is there to make sure nothing important gets missed, not to force you through every line regardless of what's already been covered.

If you're using more than one Tailored Interview Guide in the same call because the candidate could fit multiple jobs, this matters even more - you're holding two or three documents worth of questions in your head at once, and the conversation will only feel natural if you're pulling from them selectively rather than working through them like a checklist.

0. Uncover the Real Why - Before Anything Else
Do not accept the first answer. This is the question the whole call is built on.

Les is firm on this one. Every candidate gets asked why they want to move, and every candidate gets 2 to 3 follow-up questions to actually get to what's driving it. This happens first, before you sell anything - it's the foundation the rest of the conversation sits on.

You: Hey [Name], good to meet you - thanks for jumping on.

Open warmly. Your sourcer has already told them who you are, so you're picking up a conversation, not starting a new one.

You: What's making you want to make the move to Australia?

Most candidates give you a surface answer first - better money, better weather, a change of scenery. That's not the real answer, it's the doorway to it. Your job is to keep gently pushing until you get something specific and real.

Example: Candidate says: "I want to move because the money is better."

Do not accept that and move on. Ask 2 to 3 follow-up questions such as:
  • "Sure, but what would that extra money actually let you do that you can't do right now?"
  • "Is there something specific driving that - a house deposit, supporting family back home, paying off debt?"
  • "Why does that matter to you at this point in your life?"

Keep digging until you get a real, specific answer - not a generic one. This tells you whether the candidate is genuinely motivated to make this move work, and it gives you something real to reference later when you sell the opportunity and during the cool off period conversation. Log their real motivation in your notes - you'll use it again.

Lead With the Opportunity
Screening already happened. Don't repeat it, build on it.

Your sourcer has already checked the basics: trade, qualifications, citizenship, when they can move, where they want to go and their pay expectations. Asking it all again wastes the candidate's time and makes us look disorganised. You've read the transcript, so use it. Go straight into selling the role.

You: This is just a chat so I can give you a proper rundown of the role and see if it's a good fit for where you're at.
1. Run Through the Opportunity
Sell it like you actually believe in it - because the parts that are true are genuinely exciting.

Use the Selling Points section of the job brief as your foundation, but don't read it out like a list. Weave in what you learned from Part 0 - if their real "why" was providing for their kids, talk about the lifestyle and stability angle more than the pure pay number. Make it feel like it's built for them specifically.

Cover: company overview, role duties, pay and earning potential, type of work and projects, growth and progression, lifestyle in Australia.

You: From what you've told me, I think this could be a really good fit for you. It's not just a job - it's a genuine opportunity to step up, earn well, and set yourself up properly.
2. Candidate Confirms They're Keen
Don't assume interest - check it before you go deeper.

This is a short but important checkpoint. If they're hesitant here, it's worth understanding why before you invest more time in the technical and financial parts of the conversation.

You: Does that sound like something you'd want to hear more about?

If yes → continue. If no or hesitant → understand their concerns before proceeding.

3. Screening Questions
Go deeper than a checklist - but this part is genuinely more practical, and that's fine.

These questions are more transactional by nature, but keep the same conversational warmth you've had the whole call. If anything disqualifying comes up here, it's better to find out now than after the client interview is booked.

  • If you were to move, what's your timeframe?
  • Would you be moving over on your own, or bringing family with you?
  • We don't need to know your criminal history, but is there anything serious that could stop you from moving to Australia for work?
  • Any medical conditions or restrictions we should be aware of that might affect site work?
  • Do you have a valid passport with at least 6 months validity?
  • Do you have any planned vacations or commitments in the next few months?

If anything disqualifying comes up - close the call professionally before continuing.

Sub-Script - If They Say They're Not Ready Yet
This is not a dead end. It's a future booking you're locking in right now.

If the candidate says any version of "not looking to go for 3 months, 6 months, next year" - this is where you use it. The whole point is to make sure that when they are ready, they come back through Stand Up Recruitment instead of quietly self-applying for a job in Australia somewhere else. You do this by making the Grand Slam offer sound too good to walk away from - because it is.

1. Build Rapport (New)

You: Hey, did you know we're actually based here in Auckland, Takapuna?

Candidates often assume they're talking to an overseas call centre. A quick, genuine local detail like this builds trust fast, before you even get to the offer. Drop it in naturally wherever it fits in the conversation - it doesn't need to be the very first line.

2. Acknowledge

You: Hey, yeah, no worries - a lot of people we talk to aren't ready right now.

3. Reiterate the Grand Slam Offer

You: But the reason people do end up coming back through us, [Name], is because we sort you out with a job offer before you even leave New Zealand. That's going to save you a lot of stress, time, and wasted money when you get over there - because otherwise you're waiting on callbacks, going for interviews, it's just extra stress you don't want, right?

(Pause. Get the micro-yes.)

You: On top of that, most of our clients offer temporary free accommodation if you need it. That lets you save up a bit before you get sorted. If your family's moving over too, depending on the case they can come after you, once you're settled. And if you've already got somewhere to stay, that's all good too. On top of that, we'll sort your free flight over as well.

(Pause. Get the yes on each point before moving on.)

Let each of these pauses do their job. The first gets a small agreement to build on. Then take each point of the offer at its own pace - a small yes on accommodation, a small yes on family, a small yes on the flight - rather than reading it as one block.

4. The Capture Question (Updated)

You: So knowing all that, [Name], is there any reason you wouldn't come back through us when it's time to move to Australia?

This replaces the old close question. Les's preferred phrasing on purpose - "is there any reason you wouldn't" surfaces any hidden objection now instead of it showing up as a no-show later. If they say something like "well, my uncle owns a business over there and can get me a job," that's fine - still say "perfect" and move on to lock a date anyway. We still want them captured.

Alternative phrasing if it fits the conversation better: "So knowing all that, are you going to give me a call back when it's time to move to Australia?"

(Pause. Let them answer either way.)

5. Lock a Rough Date

You: Great, so roughly what month are we talking here?

Get a real month, not "sometime next year." A vague timeframe gives them no reason to call back and gives you nothing to nurture them against.

6. Confirm and Set Expectation

You: Yep, sweet, I'll pencil that in and give you a call back as we get closer. I'm gonna send you over a text message with my details, so if anything changes, you can get a hold of me before that. All good?

7. Close

You: Perfect, talk soon [Name].

After the call, update their Notice Period with the month they gave you and send the Not Ready Yet follow-up text. The folders sort them from there.

4. Financial Readiness Discussion
This exists to protect the candidate, not to interrogate them. Frame it that way.

This conversation exists to reduce dropouts after the offer - but if you frame it as "we need to check you can afford this," it feels transactional and a bit cold. Frame it instead as looking out for them: you'd rather they know the real numbers now than get blindsided later. That framing changes everything about how this lands.

Walk the candidate through the real costs - flights, accommodation, and a car, landing at roughly $5,000 all up to be comfortable.

You: Moving over costs more than most people expect. Your flight alone is $500 to $1,000. Accommodation runs $350 to $850 a week. Most people need a car within the first few weeks. So realistically, before your first pay comes in, you're looking at around $5,000 all up. (pause) Does that align with where you're at financially?

Pause before asking if it aligns. You've just given them a genuinely confronting number - rushing straight into the question doesn't give them a chance to actually process it. Let it sit for a second.

Path 1 - Clearly ready: "Perfect. We also offer a relocation package if you want it handled for you - I'll send you the info. Totally optional. Right, let's keep going..."

Path 2 - Unsure: "I appreciate you being straight with me. Use the cool off period to sit down and work out the numbers - I'll send you a cost guide."

Path 3 - Clearly not ready: "Thanks for being honest. If the finances aren't there right now it's going to be tough to make this work. Let's pause, you get yourself in a better position, and we reconnect when the timing is right."

Whichever path they land on, thank them for being straight with you. Candidates who are honest about money now are the ones who don't fall over later - reward that honesty with warmth, not disappointment.

5. Work History and Technical Assessment
Let them talk about their actual work - this is where their real expertise comes through.

This is often where candidates relax the most, because they're talking about something they know well and are proud of. Let them run with it, and use this as your opening to weave in the Commitment Questions, Role and Location Questions, and Technical Questions from your Tailored Interview Guide - going deeper rather than firing them one after another like a quiz.

You: Talk me through your experience - what have you been working on recently? Walk me through the last role or two.

Listen carefully, ask clarifying questions, and note any gaps against the job brief must-haves. As they talk, look for natural openings to bring in your Commitment and Role and Location Questions if you haven't covered them yet - then move into the 3 Technical Questions from your Tailored Interview Guide.

You: What's the thing that's going to keep you going on the tough days out there?
6. Close
Wrap up on a high note, but make sure nothing gets left hanging.

There are three things to cover here and none of them can be skipped - the cool off period, referees, and police check details. Do this in one clean sweep at the end rather than scattering it through the call. End the call with the same energy you opened it with - this is exciting for them, don't let the admin at the end flatten that.

P2 Day 2 Cool-Off Follow-Up Call

For placers. Read the tone notes, not just the words. This call happens 2 days after the internal interview. The candidate has had the cool off email and the cost guide - your job now is to find out if they've actually done the thinking, not just skimmed it. This is a real checkpoint, not a formality.

1. Open - Pick Up Where You Left Off
Warm and direct. They know why you're calling.

There's no need to re-explain the purpose of the call - they already know from the email. Open naturally and get into it.

You: Hey [Name], good to catch up again. So, had a proper think about everything?
2. Financial Readiness - Get Real Numbers, Not a Vibe
Ask for the actual figures, not a general feeling.

The cool off email specifically asked them to put real numbers to the cost guide. If they give you a vague "yeah I think I'm sweet," push once for specifics - that's the difference between someone who's actually done the exercise and someone who's just saying what they think you want to hear.

You: Are you financially ready to make the move and cover yourself for the first few weeks until your first pay comes in? Walk me through the actual numbers you've landed on.
3. Start Timing - Confirm the Real Window
You: Are you happy to start within 4 to 6 weeks?

This replaced the old "are you comfortable with the start date" question - a specific start date usually isn't locked in yet at this stage, so asking about the realistic window is more accurate and easier for them to answer honestly.

4. Location - Confirm It's Still a Yes
You: And are you still happy with the location?

Keep this short. If they've had second thoughts about the location, this is where it surfaces - don't rush past it.

5. Family and Partner - Push Past "I Mentioned It"
This is the most important question in the whole call.

This is the single most common reason placements fall apart later, so don't accept "yeah I mentioned it to them" as a real answer. You're checking whether they've actually had the conversation - the timeline, what it means day to day, all of it.

You: Have you actually sat down and had the conversation with your partner and family - not just mentioned it - about the timeline and what this means day to day?

If they're vague here, ask a genuine follow-up: "What did they say when you told them?" A real answer sounds specific. A vague one is a warning sign.

6. The Future Question - Check They're Moving Toward Something
You: Does this move make sense for where you want to be in the next few years?

The people who thrive over there are moving toward something specific, not just escaping something or chasing a change for its own sake. Listen for whether their answer is concrete or vague.

7. Close - Route Them Based on Where They Land
Don't force a yes. Read where they actually are and route accordingly.

There are four honest outcomes to this call. Don't try to talk a hesitant or cold candidate into a yes - the whole point of the cool off period is to only move forward with candidates who are genuinely ready.

If they're keen and answered everything clearly:

You: Awesome, sounds like you've thought this through properly. Let's get you moving - I'll start working on getting you in front of the client.

Move them forward to client submission.

If they're hesitant - give them 2 more days, don't push:

You: No worries, sounds like there's still a bit to work through. Let's take 2 more days - have those conversations properly and get clear on the numbers, and I'll give you a call back.

For placers. Read the tone notes, not just the words. This one's different from the others. You're not the one being interviewed and you're not the one interviewing - you're in the room to make sure the conversation between two other people goes well. That's a different skill. Most of this script is about presence and timing, not lines to recite.

1. The Open - Introduce, Then Get Out of the Way
Set the tone in one breath, then step back.

Your job in the opening is to make the introduction feel natural and then hand the floor over. Don't linger here - the longer you talk, the less this feels like the candidate and client's conversation.

You: [Client name], this is [Candidate first name]. [Candidate], this is [Client name] from [Company]. (pause) I'll let you two get into it - I'm just here to help the conversation along if needed.

Pause briefly after the introductions before handing it over - it gives both sides a beat to actually look at each other and register who's who, rather than being talked over into the next thing.

After the introduction, hand over the direction of the interview based on whether this is a new or existing client.

If it's a new client:

You: [Client name], how would you like to run this - would you want to tell [Candidate] a bit more about the company first, or would you prefer [Candidate] walks you through his experience to start?

New clients haven't worked with us before, so giving them the choice up front shows respect for how they like to operate and sets a collaborative tone from the very first minute.

If it's an existing client:

You: [Candidate], give [Client] a quick rundown on your skills and experience.

With an existing client, you already know their style and they already trust the process - you can move straight into the candidate's rundown without needing to check in on format first.

2. Reading the Room - When to Step In for the Candidate
Watch for the moment they need a lifeline, not the moment they need rescuing.

There's a difference between a candidate who's thinking before they answer and a candidate who's actually stuck. Give them the space to work through it first - jumping in too early undermines them in front of the client. Step in only when it's clear they need help getting the good stuff out.

You: [Candidate], you mentioned to me that you can do [x, y, z] - can you walk [Client] through how you approach that on site?

Frame it as prompting a memory, not correcting a wrong answer. This lets the candidate save face and still land the point.

3. Reading the Room - When to Step In for the Client
Sometimes the client needs a nudge to ask the thing they actually want to know.

Occasionally the client will circle around a concern without directly asking about it - commitment, reliability, whatever's on their mind. If you can tell there's an unspoken question sitting in the room, you can surface it for them.

You: [Candidate], [Client] is looking for someone who will commit to at least 24 months in this role - is that something you see yourself doing?

Only do this if it genuinely helps move things toward a yes. You're not creating problems that weren't there - you're clearing up ones that already exist so they can be addressed directly instead of lingering unspoken.

Setting the real stakes - say this in front of everyone:

This is a different kind of moment - you're deliberately putting a serious consequence on the table, in front of both the candidate and the client, so nobody can say later they weren't told. It also doubles as a genuine confidence check on the candidate. Slow this one right down.

You: Just one more thing while all the parties are here - [Candidate], if you get over there and you can't do what you've told us you can do, I just want to be completely clear that the client is not going to keep you around or offer you a job. (pause) Are you confident with your skills and the things that we talked about? (pause)

Pause after stating the consequence, before asking about their confidence, and then again after asking it. The first pause lets the seriousness of "not keeping you around" actually land. The second gives them real space to answer honestly rather than reflexively saying yes just to keep the momentum going. Don't run all three into one breath. This isn't about scaring the candidate - it's about making sure their yes is a real yes.

Once the candidate and client are both aligned and confident, close the loop for everyone in the room:

You: With that being said, is there any other questions from either parties?

This is a small line but it matters - it signals the conversation is wrapping up on solid ground, and it gives both sides one last genuine chance to raise anything before you move to the debrief. Don't skip it just because things feel settled.

4. The Debrief - Release the Candidate Gracefully
Get them off the call warmly and without lingering.

This moment can feel a bit awkward if you let it drag. Keep it short, warm, and clear about what happens next.

You: Thanks [Candidate] - we'll be in touch very soon. I'm just going to have a quick chat with [Client] now.
5. The Debrief - Get the Real Answer from the Client
Ask it directly. Don't hedge, and don't fill the space while they think.

Once the candidate's off the call, get straight to it. Clients appreciate directness here more than a soft lead-in - you've both just spent time on this, they know what you're about to ask.

You: What did you think? Does [Candidate] tick the boxes for you?

If they pause before answering, let them. They're forming a genuine opinion, not stalling.

6. If the Answer Is No - Extract Real Feedback
Vague feedback helps nobody. Push gently for something specific.

"Not quite right" or "just not the one" isn't useful - it doesn't tell you anything to adjust for the next candidate. Ask for something concrete, and frame it as helping you help them, not as defending your candidate.

You: Just so I can improve the next batch - what was missing for you?

Whatever they give you, log it properly in JobAdder. This is the difference between your next submission being a guess and being genuinely targeted.

7. Always Leave With a Locked-In Date
Every single debrief ends with a specific date - no exceptions.

This is non-negotiable, whether the answer was yes or no, whether they need to check with someone else or just need a day to think. "I'll get back to you" is not an answer you accept and move on from.

You: No worries - when can we expect a decision by?

Once you have that date, it's live. If it passes without word, that's what triggers the Contract Follow-Up cadence (P8 in the Placer Playbook) - so getting a real, specific date here isn't just good manners, it's what makes the whole follow-up system work.

Your part ends when you place the candidate. That automatically starts onboarding and sends the handover emails to the client and the candidate. From then on, the admin team takes over.

Full Breakdown
Step 1: Check the offer letter

Before you do anything else, run the Offer Letter Review Prompt with the offer letter and the job description.

It always tells you first whether the candidate is paid hourly or by salary, and if it's a salary, what that works out to per hour. Then it checks for:

  • Hard stops: casual employment, no guaranteed hours, no notice during probation, a salary with no paid overtime, or a pay type that isn't clear

  • Things to discuss with the client: pay that doesn't match the job description, super included in the rate, a change from hourly to salary, promised benefits missing, clauses that could move the candidate to other sites, harsh probation terms, and deductions nobody mentioned

What to do with the result:

  • Hard stop: stop. Raise it with the client before the candidate sees anything.

  • Discuss with client: go through each one. Items marked [Recommended] should usually be raised. Items marked [Optional: your call] are up to you, and are often standard wording that may never be used.

  • Offer letter looks incomplete: get the full document before going any further

  • All clear: go to Step 2

Why the pay check matters: with a salary and no paid overtime, the candidate isn't paid for any hours past their normal weekly hours, even if they work them. We've had a candidate sign one of these. Catching it here protects our candidates.

Step 2: Let the client know you've got it

Reply with the Offer Letter Received Reply template on the same day the offer letter arrives. Tell them you'll get it to the candidate that day and follow up once it's signed.

Step 4: Call the candidate and book the Welcome & Relocation Chat

As soon as the offer has gone through DocuSeal, call the candidate. This call does three things: tells them the offer has arrived, explains the 2-day cool off, and books the Welcome & Relocation Chat for 2 days' time.

  1. Share the good news. "Hey [Name], it's [Your Name] from Stand Up. Good news, we've got your letter of offer through from [Client]. This is the big one. This means [Client] wants to bring you on board in Australia."

  2. Explain the cool off. "Now before anything gets signed, we give you a 48-hour cool-down period. That's non-negotiable on our end, and it's there for you. It's your time to sit with the contract properly, talk to your partner or family, and be honest with yourself about timing, location, and what this move actually means for your life. We'd rather you take those 48 hours and be certain than rush it and regret it."

  3. Explain what's in the email. "You'll get an email from us in the next few minutes with three things in it: your letter of offer from [Client], with the pay, start date, all of it. Our terms of business, which are standard Stand Up terms. And a relocation guide. Have a proper read of that one, because it covers your bank details, your tax file number, and all the admin you'll need sorted. In 48 hours you'll be on a call with me and someone from our pastoral care team, going through that guide step by step, so if you've already read it, the call moves a lot faster."

  4. Book the Welcome & Relocation Chat. "So let's lock that in now. We're talking [today], so 48 hours takes us to [day]. What time suits you on [day]? Great, I'll send you a Teams invite for [day] at [time]. That's you, me, and our pastoral care team."

  5. End on a high. "[Name], before I let you go, we're genuinely excited to have you on board. This is a massive step and we can't wait to see you thrive over there. Talk soon!"

Once it's booked, send the Welcome & Relocation Chat Email to confirm the date, time and Teams link.

Step 5: The Welcome & Relocation Chat

A video call with you, the candidate and the pastoral care team, 2 days after the offer goes out. Every candidate has this call, whether they've signed or not.

The call is to talk through the candidate's move: their finances, their start date and their location, with the team who'll help them through it. You also go through the Relocation Guide together. If something comes up that's a dealbreaker, let the client know and work out what to do next.

Step 6: Follow up until the signed contract comes back

Don't just wait. If the signed contract hasn't come back in a reasonable time, call or message. If the client is holding things up, follow the contract follow-up cadence (P8).

Step 7: Place the candidate in JobAdder

As soon as you have the signed contract and ID, place the candidate. This starts onboarding and sends the handover emails to the client and the candidate automatically.

  1. Go to the candidate's profile in JobAdder

  2. Click the three dots menu

  3. Select Place Candidate

  4. Fill in these four fields:

Start date: the date the candidate and client agreed.

Total fee: SUR charges either a flat fee, or 15% of the candidate's full yearly pay including super. Check the job description for which one applies and for the client's super rate. Super rates differ between clients, so never assume. If the rate isn't in the job description, ask the client before placing.

Fee type Formula
Flat fee $16,000 AUD
15%, hourly rate Hourly rate × weekly hours × 52 × (1 + super rate) × 15%
15%, yearly salary, super paid on top Yearly salary × (1 + super rate) × 15%
15%, yearly salary, super already included Yearly salary × 15%

To use the super rate: 12% super means × 1.12. 11% super means × 1.11.

Example: $45 an hour, 40 hours a week, 12% super. $45 × 40 × 52 = $93,600. Then × 1.12 = $104,832. Then × 15% = $15,724.80. At 11% super, the same job comes to $93,600 × 1.11 × 15% = $15,584.40.

Application status: pick the relocation option: Full relocation package (East Coast), Full relocation package (SA), Full relocation package (WA), or No relocation package.

If you're placing someone to replace a candidate who didn't work out in the role, select Replacement instead. In the Replacement field, type the full name of the person being replaced, exactly as it shows in JobAdder.

How to Use This Guide
The ethos behind every script in this guide: every interaction - whether it's a candidate, a referee, or a client - is a conversation, not a form you're working through. Build rapport. Be genuinely curious about the person in front of you. Ask follow-up questions because you actually want to know the answer, not because a script told you to. The words matter less than the mindset you bring into them.
P7 Offer Call

For placers. Read the tone notes, not just the words. This is one of the best calls you'll ever make - you're telling someone their life is about to change. Don't rush through it because it feels like an admin step on your end. For the candidate, this is a genuinely huge moment.

1. Open / Exciting News - Let the Good News Land
This is a big deal for them. Say it like it is.

Don't bury the good news in a wall of process talk. Lead with it, plainly, and let it land before you move into anything practical.

You: Hey [Name], it's [Recruiter] from Stand Up. Good news - we've got your letter of offer through from [Client]. (pause) This is the big one. This means [Client] wants to bring you on board in Australia.

Pause after telling them the offer has come through, before explaining what it means. Give them a second to feel the news before you frame it for them.

2. Explain the Cool-Down Period - Make It About Them, Not Policy
Frame this as something we do for them, not a rule they have to follow.

If this comes across as "company policy," it feels bureaucratic. If it comes across as "we want you to be certain," it feels like we're looking out for them. Same rule, completely different impression - say it the second way.

You: Now before anything gets signed, we give you a 48-hour cool-down period. (pause) That's non-negotiable on our end, and it's there for you. It's your time to sit with the contract properly, talk to your partner or family, and be honest with yourself about timing, location, and what this move actually means for your life. We'd rather you take those 48 hours and be certain than rush it and regret it. So don't sign anything, don't decide anything right now - just go away, read it, talk about it.

Pause after stating the 48-hour period itself before explaining why it exists - the reasoning lands better once they've registered the actual timeframe first.

3. What's in the Email - Set Up the Next Call to Go Smoothly
Tell them why the relocation guide matters - it makes the next call faster for them.

Don't just list what's coming in the email. Explain why the relocation guide specifically matters, because it directly benefits them at the next call - if they've read it, that call moves faster and feels less overwhelming.

You: You'll get an email from us in the next few minutes with three things in it: your letter of offer from [Client], that's the actual job offer, pay, start date, all of it. Our terms of business, standard Stand Up terms, nothing new, just formalising us working together. And a relocation guide, this one's important. Have a proper read of it, because it covers things like your bank details, your tax file number, all the admin you'll need sorted.
You: The reason that one matters: in 48 hours, you're not just talking to me again. You'll be on a call with me and someone from our pastoral care team, and we'll be going through that relocation guide with you step by step. So if you walk in already familiar with it, that call moves a lot faster and smoother for you.
4. Book the Welcome & Relocation Chat - Lock the Date Before You Hang Up
Same rule as everywhere else - don't leave this vague.

Just like every other stage in this process, you don't end the call without a locked-in date and time. This is where you book the Welcome & Relocation Chat.

You: So let's lock that in now - we're talking [today], which means 48 hours takes us to [day]. What time suits you on [day]?
You: Great, I'll send you a Teams invite for [day] at [time]. That's you, me, and our pastoral care team - we'll walk through everything, answer any questions, and get you moving toward Australia.
You: Take the next two days, talk it through, and I'll speak to you [day].
5. Close on a High Note - Leave Them Feeling Backed
Don't end on logistics. End on how you actually feel about this.

This is a small addition but it matters - the last thing they hear from you shouldn't be a scheduling detail. It should be a genuine, human moment that tells them you're actually invested in how this goes for them.

You: [Name], before I let you go - I just want to say, we're genuinely excited to have you on board. This is a massive step and we can't wait to see you thrive over there. Talk soon!

Contract Follow-Up

Every conversation ends with a date. Not "I'll get back to you", but a specific date for a decision, a contract, or the next step.

Tell: What You Need to Know

Use this whenever a client goes quiet, whether you're waiting on a decision after the client interview or a signed contract after the offer has gone out. The same steps apply to both.

The main rule: every conversation ends with a specific date. If the client wants to think about it, your last question before you hang up is always: "When can we expect a decision by?"

The follow-up isn't a fixed ladder. It's one rule you repeat until the job is sorted or you hand it to Les.

Full Breakdown
The follow-up steps
When What to do
The date passes with nothing received Next business day: send Email 1
No reply to Email 1 Next business day: Call 1
Call 1 gives you a new date, and that one is missed too Next business day: send Email 2
No reply to Email 2 Next business day: Call 2, then stop and pass it to Les

Every time the client gives you a new date, start again from Email 1. Don't skip straight to a call. If the next step falls on a Saturday or Sunday, do it on Monday.

Example
Day What happens
Mon Interview happens. Client says: "We'll have a decision by Tuesday."
Tue Decision due. Nothing received.
Wed Email 1: check-in
Thu No reply, so Call 1. Client says: "You'll have the contract by Friday."
Fri Contract due. Nothing received.
Mon (Friday rolls over the weekend) Email 2: final email
Tue No reply, so Call 2. Stop and pass it to Les.
Email templates

  • Email 1, check-in (the business day after a missed date): use the Contract Follow-Up: Check-In template

  • Email 2, final email (when a second date is also missed and Email 1 got no reply): use the Contract Follow-Up: Urgency template

What to say on the calls

Call 1, after Email 1 gets no reply: "Hey [Name], just following up. Where are we at with the [contract/decision] for [Candidate]? Anything you need from us to get it moving?" Always get a new date before you hang up.

Call 2, after Email 2 gets no reply: "Hey [Name], wanted to get a clear answer today. Are we able to get this over the line, or do we need to have a different conversation about timing?" After this call, whatever happens, pass it to Les. Don't send any more emails or make any more calls until Les decides what's next.

How to pass it to Les

  • Usually: raise it at the next morning meeting with Les. The EA will follow it up with Les during the day and let you know what's next.

  • If it's urgent (the candidate is about to walk away, or the client finally replies partway through): don't wait for the meeting. Email Les straight away.

Keeping the candidate warm

Once Email 1 has gone out, give the candidate a quick call so they don't lose interest or take another job while you're waiting on the client.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Ending the debrief without a date

  • Sending Email 1 on the same day the date is missed. Wait until the next business day.

  • Skipping steps. Always email, then call.

  • Letting a step slip past the weekend. Do it on Monday, don't skip it.

  • Contacting the client again after Call 2. Stop and pass it to Les.
